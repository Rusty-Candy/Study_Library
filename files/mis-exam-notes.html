UNIT 1

Fundamentals Concepts of MIS

Basics of MIS · Types of MIS · Dimensions & components of IS · Benefits of MIS · IT infrastructure & its evolution · Components of IT infrastructure · Building systems in the digital firm era

1. What is MIS? ▶
MIS (Management Information System) = a system that collects, processes, stores, and distributes information to support decision-making, coordination, control, analysis, and visualization in an organization.
  • Data vs Information vs Knowledge
    • Data — raw, unorganized facts (e.g. "45, 62, 78")
    • Information — data given meaning/context (e.g. "78 = highest marks in Economics")
    • Knowledge — information combined with experience/rules to guide action
  • Purpose of MIS
    • Support routine operations (Transaction Processing)
    • Support tactical/managerial decisions
    • Support strategic, long-term planning
    • Improve coordination between departments
  • Three components of the word "MIS"
    • Management — planning, organizing, directing, controlling
    • Information — processed, meaningful data
    • System — set of interrelated components working toward a common goal
Exam tip: Always define MIS as a "triangle" of People + Technology + Organization/Process — examiners like this framing.

3. Dimensions of Information Systems ▶

An IS is NOT just technology — it has 3 dimensions (very important diagram-based question):

  • 1. Organization dimension
    • Hierarchy, functional specialties (sales, finance, HR, manufacturing)
    • Business processes, culture, standard operating procedures (SOPs)
  • 2. Management dimension
    • Managers set strategy, allocate resources, make decisions
    • Perceive challenges, create new products/services, decide how to use IT
  • 3. Technology dimension
    • Computer hardware — physical devices
    • Computer software — programs controlling hardware
    • Data management technology — organizing/managing data (DBMS)
    • Networking & telecommunications technology — linking devices/systems (incl. Internet)
    • IT services — combination of the above, managed by IT staff
Key line for exams: "Information systems literacy" = understanding the organizational, management, AND technology dimensions — not just "computer literacy" (technical knowledge alone).
3 Dimensions of an Information System
An IS only works when all 3 dimensions come together — not technology alone.
4. Components of an Information System ▶
  • Input — capturing/gathering raw data from within the organization or external environment
  • Processing — converting raw input into meaningful form (classifying, sorting, calculating)
  • Output — transferring processed information to people/activities that use it
  • Feedback — output returned to appropriate members to help evaluate/correct the input stage
  • Control — monitoring & evaluating feedback to keep system on target
Draw this as a flow: Input → Processing → Output → Feedback → (loops back to) Input
IS Component Flow
Output creates Feedback, Feedback is checked by Control, Control adjusts the next round of Input — a continuous loop.
5. Benefits of MIS ▶
  • Decision-making support
    • Timely, accurate, relevant data for structured & semi-structured decisions
  • Improves efficiency & productivity
    • Automates routine tasks; reduces manual paperwork & duplication
  • Better coordination & communication
    • Links departments; single version of truth across the firm
  • Competitive advantage
    • Faster response to market; supports innovation & differentiation
  • Control & monitoring
    • Tracks performance against goals/budgets; supports internal control & audit
  • Record-keeping & historical analysis
    • Maintains organizational memory; supports trend/forecast analysis
  • Cost reduction
    • Reduces labor costs, error costs, inventory holding costs
6. IT Infrastructure & Its Evolution ▶
IT Infrastructure = the shared technology resources (hardware, software, data, storage, networks) that provide the platform for all information systems in the firm.

Evolution — 5 Eras (very common exam question, write in sequence):

  • 1. General-purpose mainframe & minicomputer era (1959–present)
    • Highly centralized computing under professional programmers/operators; mainframes + minicomputers (e.g. IBM 1401, DEC minicomputers)
  • 2. Personal computer era (1981–present)
    • IBM PC (1981) popularized desktop computing; PC operating systems (DOS, Windows) grew; decentralization began
  • 3. Client/Server era (1983–present)
    • Desktop "clients" networked to powerful "servers" that handle processing/data storage; multi-tiered (2-tier, N-tier) client/server networks
  • 4. Enterprise computing era (1992–present)
    • Firms use TCP/IP networking to tie disparate networks into an enterprise-wide infrastructure; links to ERP, SCM, CRM systems
  • 5. Cloud & mobile computing era (2000–present)
    • Firms/individuals get computing power & software over the Internet ("cloud"); explosion of smartphones/tablets as business computing devices
Mnemonic: Main-frame → PC → Client-Server → Enterprise → Cloud/Mobile ("My Poor Cat Eats Cake")
5 Eras of IT Infrastructure
7. Components of IT Infrastructure ▶
  • Computer hardware platforms — client machines, servers, mainframes (Dell, HP, IBM, Apple)
  • Operating system platforms — Windows, Linux/UNIX, macOS, mobile OS (Android/iOS)
  • Enterprise software applications — ERP, SCM, CRM, KM systems (SAP, Oracle, Microsoft)
  • Data management & storage — DBMS software + physical storage (SAN, cloud storage)
  • Networking / telecommunications platforms — network operating systems, telecom services (wired/wireless, Internet)
  • Internet platforms — hardware/software to support company websites, intranets, extranets
  • Consulting & system integration services — vendors who integrate new tech with legacy systems
8. New Approaches for System Building in the Digital Firm Era ▶
Digital firm = an organization where nearly all significant business processes and relationships with customers, suppliers, and employees are digitally enabled and mediated.
  • Cloud computing
    • On-demand computing resources (SaaS, PaaS, IaaS) accessed over the Internet — no need to own infrastructure
  • Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA)
    • Software built as reusable "services" that different applications can call — flexible, modular systems
  • Mobile digital platform
    • Smartphones/tablets used as business platforms — mobile apps, mobile commerce
  • Agile development / Rapid Application Development (RAD)
    • Iterative, incremental building of systems instead of long traditional SDLC cycles
  • Outsourcing & end-user development
    • Getting systems built by external vendors or by business users themselves (low-code/no-code tools)
  • Big Data & Business Intelligence tools
    • Handling huge, varied, fast-moving data (Volume, Velocity, Variety) for real-time decision-making
UNIT 2

Database Management System

Objectives of database approach · Characteristics of DBMS · Data processing system · Components of DBMS packages · Database administration · ER model (conceptual)

1. Traditional File Approach — Problems ▶

Before DBMS, each application had its own separate data files → this created problems, which is WHY the database approach was needed:

  • Data redundancy — same data duplicated in many files
  • Data inconsistency — different values of "same" fact in different files
  • Program–data dependence — programs tightly tied to specific file format; any change breaks the program
  • Lack of flexibility — difficult to produce new/ad-hoc reports quickly
  • Poor security — little control over data access
  • Poor data sharing & availability — data scattered, hard to share across departments
File Approach vs Database Approach
2. Objectives of the Database Approach ▶
  • Minimize data redundancy — store each fact only once (or in controlled/known duplication)
  • Ensure data consistency — one update reflects everywhere
  • Achieve data independence — separate data structure from application programs (physical & logical independence)
  • Enable data sharing — multiple users/applications access the same data concurrently
  • Improve data security — access control, authorization levels, encryption
  • Improve data integrity — enforce accuracy & validity via constraints/rules
  • Support concurrent access — many users work on database at the same time without conflict
  • Provide backup & recovery — protect against data loss/failure
3. Characteristics / Features of DBMS ▶
DBMS = software that lets an organization create, store, organize, and access data from a single database (or set of linked databases), acting as an interface between application programs and physical data files.
  • Self-describing nature — DBMS contains not just the database but also metadata (data dictionary) describing its own structure
  • Data abstraction — provides an abstract view; hides storage/implementation details from users
  • Support for multiple views — different users see different "views" of the same data
  • Data sharing & multi-user access — with concurrency control (locking, transaction management)
  • Control of data redundancy
  • Enforces integrity constraints — e.g. primary key, foreign key, not-null, uniqueness
  • Restricts unauthorized access — via user accounts, passwords, permission levels
  • Provides backup & recovery mechanisms
  • Supports transactions with ACID properties
    • Atomicity — all-or-nothing execution
    • Consistency — DB moves from one valid state to another
    • Isolation — concurrent transactions don't interfere
    • Durability — committed changes survive failures
ACID at a Glance
4. Data Processing System ▶
Data processing = the process of converting raw data into meaningful information through a series of operations.
  • Cycle of data processing
    • Collection — gathering raw data
    • Preparation/Input — organizing, coding, validating data
    • Processing — sorting, classifying, calculating, summarizing
    • Output — presenting results as reports/documents
    • Storage — retaining data/output for future use
  • Types of data processing
    • Manual data processing — done by hand, no machines
    • Mechanical data processing — using devices like calculators, typewriters
    • Electronic Data Processing (EDP) — using computers; further divided into:
      • Batch processing — transactions accumulated & processed together at intervals (e.g. monthly payroll)
      • Online/Real-time processing — transactions processed immediately as they occur (e.g. ATM withdrawal, railway booking)
5. Components of DBMS Package ▶
  • Data Definition Language (DDL) & DDL Compiler — defines database schema/structure (CREATE, ALTER, DROP)
  • Data Manipulation Language (DML) & DML Compiler — for inserting, updating, deleting, retrieving data (INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE, SELECT)
  • Query processor / Query optimizer — interprets queries (e.g. SQL) and finds efficient execution plan
  • Data dictionary / Data catalog / System catalog — stores metadata: table names, field names, data types, relationships
  • Database engine — core service for accessing & processing data
  • Transaction manager — ensures ACID properties during concurrent transactions
  • Storage manager / File manager — manages physical storage of data on disk
  • Buffer manager — manages data transfer between disk storage and main memory
  • Report generator / Application generator — tools for creating forms & reports
  • Security & authorization module — user access control
  • Backup & recovery manager — restores database after failure/crash
DBMS: How a Query Flows
Data dictionary & security modules sit alongside every layer, checking metadata and permissions.
6. Database Administration (DBA) ▶
DBA (Database Administrator) = the person/team responsible for the technical management, control, and security of the organization's database(s).
  • Functions/Responsibilities of DBA
    • Designing & defining the database schema
    • Creating, modifying database structures (DDL operations)
    • Granting/revoking user access & managing authorization
    • Ensuring data security & privacy
    • Backup & disaster recovery planning
    • Monitoring performance & tuning the database
    • Maintaining data integrity & enforcing standards
    • Coordinating with end users, resolving conflicts over data
    • Managing the data dictionary
    • Ensuring compliance with policies/regulations
7. Entity–Relationship (ER) Model — Conceptual Design ▶
ER Model = a conceptual, graphical way of representing data requirements of an organization using entities, attributes, and relationships — the blueprint before actual database tables are created.
  • Entity — a real-world "thing" or object about which data is stored (e.g. Student, Employee, Product)
    • Entity set — collection of similar entities (e.g. all Students)
    • Weak entity — cannot be uniquely identified by its own attributes alone; depends on a "strong/owner" entity
  • Attribute — property/characteristic describing an entity (e.g. Student → Name, Roll No, DOB)
    • Simple attribute — cannot be divided further (e.g. Age)
    • Composite attribute — can be broken into sub-parts (e.g. Name → First, Last)
    • Single-valued — one value only (e.g. DOB)
    • Multi-valued — can hold multiple values (e.g. Phone Numbers) — shown with double oval/ellipse
    • Derived attribute — computed from another attribute (e.g. Age from DOB) — shown with dashed oval
    • Key attribute — uniquely identifies an entity instance (Primary Key) — underlined in diagram
  • Relationship — an association between two or more entities (e.g. Student "Enrolls in" Course)
    • Represented by a diamond shape in the ER diagram
  • Degree of relationship
    • Unary (recursive) — entity related to itself (Employee "Manages" Employee)
    • Binary — between two entities (most common) (Student–Course)
    • Ternary — among three entities (Supplier–Part–Project)
  • Cardinality (mapping) of relationships
    • One-to-One (1:1) — e.g. Employee — has — Employee ID Card
    • One-to-Many (1:M) — e.g. Department — has — Employees
    • Many-to-Many (M:N) — e.g. Students — enroll in — Courses

ER Diagram Notation (for drawing in exam):

  • Rectangle = Entity
  • Oval/Ellipse = Attribute
  • Diamond = Relationship
  • Lines = connect entities to their attributes and to relationships
  • Double rectangle = weak entity; double diamond = identifying relationship
Exam tip: Practice drawing a small ER diagram — e.g. "Library System": entities Book, Member, Librarian; relationship "Borrows" between Member and Book (M:N), with attributes like Book(ISBN, Title), Member(MemberID, Name).
Notation Legend
Worked Example: Library System ER Diagram
Underlined attribute = primary key. M and N near the lines show the many-to-many cardinality of "Borrows".
UNIT 3

Information System Applications

MIS applications · DSS & GDSS · DSS in E-enterprise · KMS & KBES · Enterprise Model & E-Business · E-Commerce, E-Communication · BPR

1. MIS Applications (by functional area) ▶
  • Finance & Accounting Information Systems — budgeting, ledger, cash flow forecasting, financial reporting
  • Human Resource Information Systems (HRIS) — payroll, recruitment, training, performance appraisal
  • Marketing/Sales Information Systems — sales forecasting, CRM, market research analysis
  • Manufacturing/Production Information Systems — inventory control, production scheduling, quality control
  • Supply Chain Management (SCM) Systems — coordinate flow of goods/info between suppliers, manufacturers, distributors
  • Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) — integrates all functional areas into one system
2. Decision Support System (DSS) ▶
DSS = a computer-based information system that helps managers make decisions for semi-structured and unstructured problems by combining data, sophisticated analytical models, and user-friendly interfaces.
  • Characteristics of DSS
    • Supports semi-structured/unstructured decisions (unlike TPS which handles structured tasks)
    • Combines models + data + user judgment
    • Interactive, user-friendly, flexible ("what-if" analysis)
    • Supports rather than replaces managerial judgment
  • Components of DSS
    • Database (data management) subsystem — internal & external data
    • Model base (model management) subsystem — statistical, financial, simulation models
    • User interface (dialogue management) subsystem — allows interaction between user & system
    • (Sometimes) Knowledge management subsystem
Anatomy of a DSS
DSS supports — never replaces — the manager's own judgement.
3. Group Decision Support System (GDSS) ▶
GDSS = an interactive computer-based system that supports a group of decision-makers working together on a semi-structured/unstructured problem, especially useful for group meetings/brainstorming.
  • Features
    • Allows parallel input from multiple people simultaneously (reduces "production blocking")
    • Anonymity of input — reduces fear of criticism, encourages honest opinions
    • Tools for idea generation, voting, ranking, prioritizing
    • Can be used for same-time/same-place or different-time/different-place meetings
  • Benefits
    • Improves group decision quality & participation
    • Saves time; reduces dominance by a few vocal members
    • Creates a documented record of discussion
4. DSS Applications in E-Enterprise ▶
  • E-enterprise = an organization that uses digital technology extensively to conduct business, coordinate with partners, and reach customers
  • Application areas of DSS within E-enterprise:
    • Customer analytics/CRM analytics — analyzing customer buying patterns for targeted offers
    • Dynamic pricing decisions — e.g. airline/e-commerce price optimization
    • Supply chain & logistics optimization — route planning, inventory decisions
    • Financial risk analysis & forecasting
    • Web analytics & digital marketing decisions — analyzing website traffic/conversion data
5. Knowledge Management System (KMS) & Knowledge-Based Expert System (KBES) ▶
KMS = systems that support the creation, organization, storage, sharing, and application of an organization's knowledge assets.
  • Types of knowledge
    • Tacit knowledge — personal, experience-based, hard to document (skills, intuition)
    • Explicit knowledge — documented, codified knowledge (manuals, reports, databases)
  • KMS processes — knowledge capture → storage → sharing/dissemination → application
    KMS Process Flow
  • Knowledge-Based Expert System (KBES / Expert System)
    Expert System = an AI-based system that mimics the decision-making ability of a human expert in a specific, narrow domain, using a knowledge base and inference rules.
    • Knowledge base — facts & rules (IF-THEN) about the domain
    • Inference engine — reasoning mechanism that applies rules to facts to reach conclusions
    • User interface — allows user to interact/query the system
    • Examples: medical diagnosis systems (MYCIN), tax-advisory systems, credit-approval systems
6. Enterprise Model System & E-Business ▶
  • Enterprise System (ERP)
    • Integrates key business processes (finance, HR, manufacturing, sales) into one unified software system with a single database
    • Enables information flow across departments; eliminates data silos
    • Examples: SAP, Oracle ERP, Microsoft Dynamics
  • E-Business (Electronic Business)
    E-Business = the use of digital technology and the Internet to run the core processes of a business — broader than e-commerce (includes internal processes, not just buying/selling).
    • Covers: e-commerce + internal operations (e.g. supply chain, CRM, ERP linked digitally)
7. E-Commerce ▶
E-Commerce = buying and selling of goods/services, or transmitting funds/data, over an electronic network (mainly the Internet).
  • Types of e-commerce (by parties involved)
    • B2C (Business-to-Consumer) — e.g. Amazon, Flipkart
    • B2B (Business-to-Business) — e.g. IndiaMART, Alibaba
    • C2C (Consumer-to-Consumer) — e.g. OLX, eBay
    • C2B (Consumer-to-Business) — e.g. freelancing platforms, Upwork
    • B2G / G2C (with Government) — e.g. e-filing of taxes, government e-marketplace (GeM)
    E-Commerce Types — Who Sells to Whom
    B2G/G2C sits alongside these four, with government as the buyer or seller.
  • Digital goods vs digital markets
    • Digital markets — marketplaces where transactions occur online (e.g. Amazon marketplace) — reduce information asymmetry, transaction costs, "search costs"
    • Digital goods — goods that can be delivered digitally (e.g. e-books, music, software, movies) — near-zero cost of reproduction/distribution
  • Benefits of e-commerce
    • Global reach, 24×7 availability, lower operating costs
    • Personalization & customization of offers
    • Reduced "menu costs" (cost of changing prices) and "search costs" for buyers
8. E-Communication ▶
  • E-Communication = exchange of information electronically — email, instant messaging, video conferencing, social media, VoIP
  • Enables: collaboration across geographies, faster decision-making, remote/virtual teams, reduced travel costs
  • Tools: Email, Zoom/Teams, intranets, enterprise social networks, EDI (Electronic Data Interchange)
9. Business Process Reengineering (BPR) ▶
BPR = the radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical measures of performance — cost, quality, service, and speed — often enabled by IT.
  • Key features
    • Radical redesign, not incremental improvement (contrast with continuous/TQM improvement)
    • Focus on processes, not departments/functions
    • Driven and enabled heavily by Information Technology
  • Steps in BPR
    • 1. Identify processes for redesign
    • 2. Analyze existing ("as-is") processes
    • 3. Design the new ("to-be") process
    • 4. Implement the reengineered process
    • 5. Continuously evaluate
  • Benefits — reduced cost, faster cycle time, improved quality, better customer service
  • Risks — high failure rate, employee resistance, disruption during transition
BPR Cycle: As-Is → To-Be
Evaluation feeds straight back into the next round of process identification.
UNIT 4

Managing Projects

Objectives of PM · Fundamentals of PM with agile · SCRUM (roles & meetings) · User stories · Project risk · Ethical, social, political issues in the information era

1. Objectives of Project Management ▶
Project Management = the application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to plan, execute, and control project activities to meet defined requirements.
  • Complete the project within scope (deliver what was promised — no more, no less)
  • Complete within time (schedule)
  • Complete within budget (cost)
  • Meet the required quality standards
  • Manage risk effectively throughout the project
  • Ensure stakeholder satisfaction
  • Optimal use of resources (people, money, time, equipment)
The "Triple Constraint" / Iron Triangle: Scope – Time – Cost (Quality sits at the centre; changing one affects the others)
The Iron Triangle
Pull on any one corner and the other two — and the quality at the centre — feel it.
2. Fundamentals of Project Management for IS ▶
  • Why IS projects need special PM attention
    • High failure rate — over budget, over time, or fail to meet requirements
    • Rapidly changing requirements & technology
    • Intangible deliverable (software) makes progress hard to measure
  • Traditional approach — Waterfall / SDLC
    • Sequential phases: Planning → Analysis → Design → Implementation → Testing → Maintenance
    • Each phase completed fully before the next starts; rigid, document-heavy
    • Weakness: hard to accommodate changing requirements mid-project
  • Modern approach — Agile methodology
    Agile = an iterative, flexible approach to project management that breaks work into small increments ("sprints") with continuous collaboration, feedback, and adaptation.
    • Core values (from the Agile Manifesto):
      • Individuals & interactions over processes & tools
      • Working software over comprehensive documentation
      • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
      • Responding to change over following a rigid plan
    • Delivers work in short, iterative cycles → early & continuous delivery of value
    • Encourages flexibility — welcomes changing requirements even late in development
    • Emphasizes close, daily collaboration between business people & developers
  • Waterfall vs Agile
    AspectWaterfallAgile
    ApproachSequential, linearIterative, incremental
    RequirementsFixed upfrontEvolve continuously
    Customer involvementMainly at start & endContinuous throughout
    FlexibilityLowHigh
    DeliveryOne final product at the endFrequent working increments
Waterfall vs Agile — Shape of the Work
3. Introduction to SCRUM ▶
SCRUM = the most widely used Agile framework — an iterative process where work is done in fixed-length cycles called Sprints (usually 2–4 weeks) to deliver a "potentially shippable" product increment.
  • Key Scrum artifacts
    • Product Backlog — prioritized master list of all features/requirements (user stories) needed
    • Sprint Backlog — subset of product backlog items selected for the current sprint
    • Increment / Product Increment — the working, usable product output at end of each sprint
  • Scrum values
    • Commitment, Courage, Focus, Openness, Respect
The Sprint Cycle
Review looks at the PRODUCT; Retrospective looks at the PROCESS — the classic exam mix-up.
4. Roles in SCRUM ▶
  • Product Owner
    • Represents stakeholders/customers; the "voice of the business"
    • Owns and prioritizes the Product Backlog
    • Defines what needs to be built and in what order (maximizes product value)
  • Scrum Master
    • Facilitator/coach for the team — NOT a traditional "boss"
    • Ensures the team follows Scrum practices; removes obstacles/blockers
    • Shields the team from outside interference; runs Scrum ceremonies
  • Development Team
    • Cross-functional, self-organizing group who actually build the product increment
    • Typically small (5–9 members); collectively responsible for delivery
3 Roles: Product Owner – Scrum Master – Development Team ("PSD")
The Scrum Team
5. Scrum Meetings (Ceremonies/Events) ▶
  • Sprint Planning — held at start of sprint; team selects backlog items & plans how to deliver them
  • Daily Scrum / Daily Stand-up — short (~15 min) daily meeting; each member answers:
    • What did I do yesterday?
    • What will I do today?
    • Are there any blockers/obstacles?
  • Sprint Review — held at end of sprint; team demonstrates the completed increment to stakeholders for feedback
  • Sprint Retrospective — team reflects on the sprint itself (what went well/badly) to improve the process going forward
Exam tip: Don't confuse Sprint Review (about the PRODUCT) with Sprint Retrospective (about the PROCESS/team).
6. User Stories ▶
User Story = a short, simple description of a feature told from the perspective of the end user, used in agile to capture requirements.
  • Standard format
    • "As a [type of user], I want [some goal] so that [some reason/benefit]."
    • Example: "As a student, I want to view my exam results online so that I don't have to visit college."
  • INVEST criteria for a good user story
    • Independent — can be developed separately from other stories
    • Negotiable — details can be discussed, not a rigid contract
    • Valuable — delivers value to the user/customer
    • Estimable — team can estimate the effort required
    • Small — small enough to complete within a sprint
    • Testable — clear enough to verify when it's "done"
  • Acceptance criteria — conditions that must be met for the story to be considered complete/"done"
7. Project Risk & Controlling Risk Factors ▶
  • Types of project risk in IS projects
    • Technical risk — technology doesn't work as expected, integration issues
    • Schedule risk — project running behind timeline
    • Cost/budget risk — project exceeding allocated budget
    • Scope risk / Scope creep — uncontrolled expansion of project requirements
    • Resource risk — lack of skilled staff, staff turnover
    • Organizational risk — lack of management support, resistance to change
    • External risk — vendor failure, regulatory/legal changes, market changes
  • Risk management process (steps)
    • 1. Risk identification — list potential risks
    • 2. Risk assessment/analysis — evaluate probability & impact of each risk
    • 3. Risk prioritization — rank risks (e.g. using a probability–impact matrix)
    • 4. Risk response planning — decide strategy:
      • Avoid — eliminate the risk cause
      • Mitigate — reduce probability/impact
      • Transfer — shift risk to third party (e.g. insurance, outsourcing)
      • Accept — acknowledge and monitor if impact is minor
    • 5. Risk monitoring & control — track identified risks & watch for new ones throughout the project
    Risk Management Process
    Monitoring feeds back into identifying new risks — the process runs continuously, not just once.
  • How Agile/Scrum controls risk
    • Short sprints → problems surface early, not at the end
    • Continuous stakeholder feedback reduces scope/requirement risk
    • Daily stand-ups surface blockers quickly
8. Ethical, Social & Political Issues in the Information Era ▶

Framework (5 moral dimensions of the information age — good for long-answer questions):

  • 1. Information rights & obligations (Privacy)
    • What rights do individuals have over their own personal data?
    • Issues: data collection without consent, surveillance, profiling, data breaches
  • 2. Property rights & obligations (Intellectual Property)
    • Protecting software, digital content via copyright, patents, trade secrets
    • Issues: piracy, plagiarism, unauthorized copying/distribution of digital goods
  • 3. Accountability & control
    • Who is responsible when an information system causes harm (e.g. faulty algorithm, data leak)?
  • 4. System quality
    • What standard of data/system quality should be demanded to protect rights and safety?
    • Issues: software bugs, poor data quality leading to wrong decisions
  • 5. Quality of life
    • Social effects of IT — job displacement/automation, digital divide, health effects (screen addiction, RSI), loss of face-to-face community
5 Moral Dimensions of the Information Age
A ready-made framework for long-answer ethics questions.
  • Social issues
    • Digital divide — unequal access to technology between rich/poor, urban/rural
    • Job displacement — automation replacing routine jobs
    • Cybercrime & cyberbullying
    • Changing nature of work — remote work, gig economy
  • Political issues
    • Data localization & sovereignty — where data is stored/who governs it
    • Regulation of tech giants — antitrust, content moderation laws
    • Cybersecurity & national security — protecting critical infrastructure
    • Election interference & misinformation via digital platforms
    • Data protection laws — e.g. GDPR (EU), IT Act/DPDP Act (India)
  • Ethical principles used to analyze IS dilemmas
    • Golden Rule — treat others as you'd want to be treated
    • Kant's Categorical Imperative — if an action isn't right for everyone, it's not right for anyone
    • Risk Aversion Principle — choose the action with lowest risk/harm
    • "No free lunch" rule — assume all property/information has an owner unless stated otherwise
