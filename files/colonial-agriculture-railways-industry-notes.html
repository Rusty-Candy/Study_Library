Agriculture under Colonial Rule

01 — LAND REVENUE

Land Revenue Systems

The British restructured land rights across India through three major land revenue settlements, each creating a distinct class of intermediaries and a distinct set of agrarian relations.

Table 1.1 — The three land revenue systems compared

RegionsBengal, Bihar, Orissa, parts of Madras/VaranasiMadras, Bombay Presidency, AssamParts of North-Western Provinces, Punjab, Central India

Exam angle
A frequent MCQ trap: the Permanent Settlement fixed the state's revenue demand on the zamindar forever — it did not fix the rent the zamindar could charge tenants, which is exactly why rack-renting and sub-infeudation flourished under Zamindari areas.
02 — AGRARIAN STRUCTURE

Agrarian Structure & Land Relations

Key structural features that emerged

  • Sub-infeudation — zamindars leased out revenue-collection rights to intermediaries (under-tenures), who in turn sub-let further, creating layered chains of rent-extracting intermediaries between the state and the actual cultivator
  • Absentee landlordism — many zamindars lived away from their estates, extracting rent with little investment in land improvement
  • Rack-renting — since revenue to the state was fixed (Zamindari areas) but rent to tenants was not, zamindars had every incentive to raise tenant rents as high as possible
  • Rise of tenancy & insecurity of tenure — occupancy rights were poorly protected until later Tenancy Acts (e.g., Bengal Tenancy Act 1885) attempted (with limited success) to protect occupancy ryots
  • Fragmentation and sub-division of holdings — driven by population pressure and inheritance practices, reducing average farm size over time
  • Commercialization-driven land alienation — cash-crop dependence and debt often led to land passing from cultivators to moneylenders/merchants (especially where land became a marketable/mortgageable commodity under British law, unlike traditional customary tenure)
Sub-infeudation
The proliferation of multiple layers of rent-receiving intermediaries between the actual cultivator and the state — each layer extracting a share of the surplus, without necessarily investing in production, one of the most criticised features of the Permanent Settlement.
03 — CREDIT

Agricultural Markets & Institutions: Credit

The centrality of the village moneylender
In the absence of institutional/formal credit, the village moneylender (sahukar) was the dominant — often the only — source of rural credit, financing both consumption needs (between harvests) and production inputs, typically at very high interest rates.

Why did rural indebtedness deepen under colonial rule?

  • Fixed/rigid land revenue demand (especially Zamindari) had to be paid in cash, in time, regardless of harvest outcome — forcing distress borrowing in bad years
  • Monetization & commercialization of agriculture increased cultivators' exposure to price risk and the need for cash
  • Making land a transferable/mortgageable commodity under British legal reforms (unlike more protected customary tenures) allowed moneylenders to seize land on default — a new channel of dispossession
  • Absence of formal/institutional rural banking for most of the colonial period left moneylenders as monopolistic credit providers, able to charge exorbitant, often usurious, interest rates

Institutional responses

Table 3.1 — Colonial-era attempts to address rural indebtedness
MeasureNatureEffectiveness
Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act, 1879Restricted moneylenders' ability to seize peasant land in the Deccan (following the Deccan Riots of 1875)Limited — moneylenders adapted via other legal/informal mechanisms
Cooperative Credit Societies Act, 1904Introduced village cooperative credit societies as an institutional alternative to moneylendersSlow to spread; often captured by richer peasants; limited outreach to the poorest
Land Alienation Acts (Punjab, 1900, and similar)Restricted transfer/sale of agricultural land to non-agriculturist castes (chiefly to curb moneylender acquisition)Partial success in specific regions; did not solve the underlying credit shortage
Exam angle
The Deccan Riots (1875) — peasant uprisings against moneylenders in Maharashtra — are a classic reference point, directly leading to the Deccan Agriculturists' Relief Act and highlighting how commercialization-plus-fixed-revenue-plus-absent-credit institutions combined to fuel rural distress.
04 — COMMERCE

Commercialization of Agriculture

Commercialization of Agriculture
The shift from subsistence food-crop cultivation toward cash/commercial crops (cotton, jute, indigo, tea, opium, oilseeds, sugarcane) grown primarily for sale in domestic and — crucially — export markets, rather than for the cultivator's own consumption.

Drivers

  • Growing demand from British/European industry for raw materials (cotton for Lancashire textile mills, jute for packaging, indigo as a dye)
  • Expansion of railways, which linked previously isolated agricultural regions to ports and international markets (see Section 8)
  • The need for cash to pay fixed land revenue demands, pushing cultivators toward marketable crops
  • Deliberate colonial policy encouragement of specific export crops (e.g., indigo under coercive contracts, opium under state monopoly)
"Commercialization" was not always "modernization"
Unlike a voluntary market-driven shift reflecting genuine comparative advantage and rising farmer welfare, much of colonial commercialization was driven by revenue compulsion and coercion (e.g., the indigo system in Bengal, forced cultivation contracts) — historians distinguish this "forced commercialization" from a healthy market-integration process, and link it to increased vulnerability to price fluctuations and famine (Section 7).

Consequences

  • Increased regional specialization and integration into world commodity markets (and thus exposure to global price volatility, e.g., the collapse of cotton/jute prices during the Great Depression)
  • Reduced acreage devoted to food crops in some regions, contributing to food insecurity
  • Growth of a class of rural traders/middlemen alongside the moneylender
05 — TECHNOLOGY

Agricultural Technology & Irrigation

Broad technological stagnation
Despite some improvements (railways, and expansion of irrigated area in specific tracts), Indian agriculture as a whole saw little technological transformation during the colonial period — cultivation techniques, implements, and seed varieties remained largely traditional; yields per acre stayed low and largely stagnant.

Irrigation — the partial exception

  • Major canal systems were built in select regions — notably Punjab (the "canal colonies") and parts of the United Provinces/Madras — significantly expanding irrigated acreage there
  • But irrigation investment was geographically uneven: large tracts (especially in eastern and central India) remained rain-fed and vulnerable to monsoon failure
  • Motivation was often as much about opening new revenue-generating, commercially cultivable tracts (canal colonies for cash crops) as about food security

Why did technology stagnate?

  • Low and uncertain returns to investment for small, indebted, insecure-tenure cultivators — little incentive/capacity to invest in land improvement
  • Colonial state investment priorities favoured railways and irrigation works serving export/revenue interests over broad-based agricultural research/extension
  • Absence of an institutional credit system to finance modern inputs
  • Fragmented, small holdings limiting scope for mechanization
06 — PERFORMANCE

Trends in Agricultural Performance & Productivity

George Blyn's Estimates (a landmark quantitative study)
Economic historian George Blyn's pioneering statistical reconstruction (covering roughly 1891-1947) remains the standard reference for colonial-era agricultural output trends, showing broadly stagnant to very slowly growing aggregate output, with output per capita declining due to population growth outpacing output growth.
Table 6.1 — Broad findings (illustrative, per Blyn-type estimates, 1891-1947)
IndicatorApproximate trend
All-crop output growth (aggregate)~0.4% per annum (very slow)
Foodgrain output growthNear-stagnant, or even mildly declining in per-capita terms
Non-foodgrain (commercial crop) output growthRelatively higher, reflecting commercialization
Yield per acreLargely stagnant across most regions
Regional variationSharp — e.g., Punjab (irrigated, canal colonies) performed markedly better than Bengal/Eastern India
Exam angle
The key distinguishing point often tested: total agricultural output grew very slowly, but foodgrain output per capita actually declined over the late colonial period because population growth outpaced this slow output growth — a central piece of evidence in debates about colonial-era welfare/living standards.

Explanations offered for stagnation

  • Exploitative land revenue and tenurial systems reducing cultivator incentives to invest
  • Fragmentation of holdings
  • Lack of technological change/institutional credit (Section 5)
  • Diversion of good land to non-food commercial crops in some regions
  • Insufficient state investment in agricultural research and extension, relative to railways/irrigation-for-export priorities

Causes of famine — the analytical debate

Food Availability Decline (FAD) view
Traditional explanation: famines were caused primarily by an absolute shortfall in food supply — crop failure due to drought/flood, reducing the total food available.
Amartya Sen's Entitlement Approach (challenging FAD)
Sen argued that famines — especially the 1943 Bengal Famine — occurred even without a dramatic overall decline in food availability. Instead, famine resulted from a collapse in specific groups' "entitlements" (their ability to command/acquire food through production, wages, or market exchange) — e.g., wartime inflation, speculative hoarding, disrupted rice imports from Burma (after its 1942 occupation by Japan), and priority procurement for the war effort left agricultural labourers and other non-food-producing groups unable to buy food even though aggregate supply had not collapsed as sharply as believed.
Exam angle
Sen's Entitlement Approach (from Poverty and Famines, 1981) is one of the most frequently tested ideas in this topic — the key point is that famine is about failure of entitlement/access, not necessarily an absolute food-availability collapse; this shifted famine analysis from a purely agronomic question to one about markets, wages, and political economy.

Colonial policy response

  • Successive Famine Commissions (1880, 1898, 1901) recommended famine codes, relief works, and (limited) irrigation/railway investment for famine-prone tracts
  • Railways were partly justified as a famine-prevention tool (enabling grain movement to deficit areas) — though their actual famine-mitigation effect is debated, since railways also facilitated grain exports out of deficit regions when prices were more favourable elsewhere
  • Relief measured largely through public works (wage employment) rather than direct food distribution for much of the period, reflecting laissez-faire administrative philosophy (notably under Lord Lytton during the 1876-78 famine, criticised for prioritising fiscal orthodoxy over relief)

Railways and Industry

08 — RAILWAYS

Railways

The Guarantee System
Railway construction (from 1853 onward) was financed largely through private British companies, whose capital investment was guaranteed a fixed minimum rate of return (typically 5%) by the colonial government, regardless of actual profitability — a scheme designed to attract British capital with minimal risk to investors, but at significant fiscal cost to Indian taxpayers when returns fell short.

Motives behind railway construction (debated)

Table 8.1 — Stated vs critical view of railway motives
Stated/official motivesNationalist/critical view
Facilitate trade and economic developmentPrimarily served to move raw materials to ports for export and manufactured British goods into the Indian interior
Enable famine relief via grain movementAlso enabled grain exports from famine-affected regions when export prices were attractive, sometimes worsening local scarcity
Administrative/military efficiency and troop movementReinforced colonial control and rapid deployment of British forces
A modernizing, "civilizing" infrastructure investmentGuarantee system was a lucrative, low-risk investment vehicle for British capital, with the fiscal burden of shortfalls borne by Indian revenues

Economic impact of railways — a debated legacy

  • Positive linkage effects: reduced transport costs, integrated regional grain markets (reducing local price volatility in normal years), stimulated some backward linkages (e.g., demand for coal, some engineering activity)
  • Weak forward/backward linkages for Indian industry: most railway equipment (locomotives, rails, rolling stock) was imported from Britain rather than domestically manufactured, so the railways' potential to stimulate a domestic capital-goods/engineering industry (as happened in some other countries) was largely not realised under colonial policy
  • Fiscal burden: the guarantee system's shortfall payments were a drain on Indian public finances
  • Facilitated the commercialization of agriculture (Section 4) and arguably accelerated the de-industrialization process by easing the penetration of British manufactured goods (especially textiles) into interior markets (Section 9)
Exam angle
The railways are a classic "double-edged" topic: they did integrate markets and reduce transport costs (a real economic benefit), but critics (Bipan Chandra and other nationalist historians) stress that their linkage effects were captured mostly outside India (British locomotive/steel industry) rather than stimulating Indian industrialization — contrast this with how railways spurred domestic heavy industry in, say, the United States or Germany.
09 — DE-INDUSTRIALIZATION

The De-Industrialization Debate

De-industrialization thesis
The argument that India's traditional handicraft/artisanal manufacturing (especially handloom textiles) suffered a severe decline during the 19th century, as colonial trade policy and machine-made British imports destroyed indigenous industry, converting India from a manufacturing exporter into primarily a raw-material exporter/importer of manufactures.

Mechanisms cited by proponents

  • One-way free trade: Britain imposed near-free entry for its machine-made textiles into India, while Indian handicraft exports to Britain faced high tariffs — an asymmetric "free trade" that favoured British industry
  • Decline of princely courts and traditional patrons of luxury handicrafts after the establishment of British political dominance
  • Railways easing the penetration of cheap machine-made goods into previously isolated rural markets, undercutting local artisans
  • Displaced artisans pushed into agriculture — swelling the already-large agricultural workforce, contributing to the "ruralization"/increasing pressure on land in the agrarian economy
Critics of the strong de-industrialization thesis (Morris D. Morris and others)
Question the reliability of the underlying data (occupation census categories were often ambiguous/inconsistent across years), argue that the decline of some traditional crafts was a normal consequence of economic change (comparable to craft decline during industrialization elsewhere), and note that certain crafts and some new small-scale/cottage industries also grew during this period, complicating a simple decline narrative.
Bipan Chandra & nationalist historians' response
Maintain that even with data limitations, the qualitative and regional evidence — collapse of famous centres like Dhaka muslin weaving, sharp fall in yarn/cloth exports alongside a rise in raw cotton exports — supports a genuine, colonially-induced de-industrialization, distinct from "normal" structural change, because it was not accompanied by compensating growth in modern industry within India (unlike industrializing economies elsewhere where craft decline coincided with factory-sector growth).
Exam angle
This is one of the most debated topics in Indian economic history — CUET PG may test it as a straightforward factual question (who argued what) or ask you to evaluate the debate. Key names to remember: Bipan Chandra, Amiya Bagchi (pro-de-industrialization thesis) vs Morris D. Morris (skeptic, data-critical view); also associated with debates around Daniel Thorner and the occupational census evidence.
10 — ENTREPRENEURSHIP

Evolution of Entrepreneurial & Industrial Structure

The Managing Agency System
A distinctive organisational form of colonial Indian industry: a "Managing Agency" — typically a British (and later also Indian) trading/business house — would manage one or more industrial companies (raising capital, controlling operations, marketing) in exchange for management fees/commissions, often without owning a majority equity stake — concentrating enormous control in a few agency houses (e.g., Andrew Yule, Bird & Co., later Tata Sons, Birla Brothers).

Communities that emerged as industrial entrepreneurs

Exam angle
Bombay's cotton textile industry was pioneered largely by Indian (Parsi and Gujarati) capital from very early on, while Calcutta's jute industry remained predominantly under British managing agency control throughout the colonial period — a key regional/community contrast frequently tested.

Key features of the industrial structure

  • Regional concentration: modern industry clustered heavily in Bombay (cotton textiles) and Calcutta/Bengal (jute), rather than spreading broadly
  • Heavy reliance on managing agencies for capital mobilisation and control, given underdeveloped formal capital markets
  • Gradual "Indianization" of industrial ownership over time, particularly accelerating after World War I and through the interwar period (Section 11)
11 — INTERWAR INDUSTRY

Nature of Industrialization in the Interwar Period (1919–1939)

Discriminating Protection
A tariff policy adopted from 1923 (following the Indian Fiscal Commission, 1921-22) granting selective, "discriminating" tariff protection to specific Indian industries (notably cotton textiles, iron & steel, sugar, paper, matches) deemed to have genuine prospects of becoming efficient/competitive — a significant, if limited and grudging, departure from the earlier free-trade orthodoxy.

Key features of interwar industrial growth

  • Relatively faster industrial growth than the pre-WWI period, aided by discriminating protection and reduced British competition during/after WWI
  • Growth of Indian-owned enterprise and capital, especially in cotton textiles (Bombay/Ahmedabad) and new sectors like sugar, cement, paper, and (from the 1910s-20s) iron & steel (TISCO, established 1907, expanded significantly in this period)
  • Continued narrow industrial base: growth concentrated in a handful of consumer-goods industries (textiles, sugar) rather than a broad-based capital-goods/heavy industrial sector
  • World War I (1914-18) gave a temporary boost to Indian industry by cutting off British imports and creating wartime demand, but also strained the economy through inflation and resource diversion
  • The Great Depression (1929-33) hit agricultural export prices hard (cotton, jute) but had a comparatively milder/mixed effect on some protected domestic industries
Exam angle
"Discriminating protection" (post-1923) is distinct from full protectionism — it was selectively granted industry-by-industry after Tariff Board inquiries, and often came with conditions (e.g., "imperial preference" clauses favouring British goods over other foreign competitors even within the "protected" framework) — a nuance worth remembering.
12 — CONSTRAINTS

Constraints to Industrial Breakthrough

Despite pockets of growth, colonial India never achieved a broad-based industrial "takeoff." Historians point to several interlocking constraints.

Table 12.1 — Categories of constraint
ConstraintExplanation
Colonial trade & tariff policyFree-trade orthodoxy for most of the period suppressed infant-industry protection; even post-1923 "discriminating protection" was limited and selective
Narrow domestic marketLow agricultural incomes and mass poverty (linked to agrarian stagnation, Section 6) kept purchasing power — and hence demand for manufactured goods — very limited
Capital market underdevelopmentAbsence of well-developed banking/capital markets forced reliance on managing agencies and merchant capital, restricting the scale and sectoral spread of investment
Weak capital-goods sectorMachinery, equipment (including railway equipment) was overwhelmingly imported rather than domestically produced — no self-sustaining industrial "linkage" chain developed
Government procurement & policy biasGovernment purchases (railways, public works) often favoured British suppliers over Indian manufacturers
Skill & technical education gapsLimited state investment in technical/scientific education constrained the supply of skilled engineers and managers
Entrepreneurial constraintsIndian capital was concentrated in trade/moneylending for much of the period; the transition to industrial entrepreneurship, while real (Section 10), took time and remained regionally concentrated
Exam angle
A "systemic" answer is usually expected here — no single constraint alone explains the absence of a broad industrial breakthrough; examiners often want you to connect agrarian stagnation (weak demand) with trade policy (external competition) and capital-market weakness (finance) as a mutually reinforcing set of constraints.
13 — LABOUR

Labour Relations

Nature of the industrial workforce

  • Predominantly migrant labour, drawn from famine-prone and agriculturally stressed rural areas (e.g., Bombay's textile mill workers migrating from the Konkan region; Calcutta's jute mill workers substantially from Bihar/UP/Odisha)
  • Retained strong rural ties — workers often moved back and forth between village and factory (a "circular migration" pattern), reflecting land as a fallback and the insecurity of urban industrial employment
  • Jobber system: a labour intermediary (the "jobber" or "sardar") recruited, supervised, and often exploited workers on behalf of factory owners/managing agencies, acting as an informal but powerful labour-control mechanism, especially in Bombay's textile mills

Working conditions

  • Long working hours, low wages, unsafe/unsanitary factory conditions, extensive use of child labour and women workers (especially in textile mills), for much of the 19th century
  • Minimal legal protection for most of the colonial period

Evolution of labour legislation

Table 13.1 — Key colonial-era labour laws
LegislationKey provision
Factories Act, 1881 (and amendments 1891, 1911, 1922, 1934)Progressive (if slow and limited) restrictions on child labour, working hours, and factory safety — driven partly by British manufacturers' lobbying to reduce the cost advantage of unregulated Indian mills, not purely by welfare concern
Trade Unions Act, 1926First formal legal recognition of trade unions in India
Trade Disputes Act, 1929Provided mechanisms for conciliation in industrial disputes; also restricted certain strike actions

Rise of the trade union movement

  • Early, localized strikes and protests (Bombay textile strikes from the late 19th/early 20th century)
  • Formation of the All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) in 1920 — the first national-level trade union federation, with early involvement of nationalist leaders
  • Growing labour unrest and strikes through the 1920s-30s, intersecting with the broader nationalist movement
Exam angle
Note the layered motivation behind colonial factory legislation: it is often (correctly) framed not simply as benevolent regulation, but partly as a response to lobbying by British manufacturers (in Lancashire) who wanted to curb the competitive cost advantage that unregulated (long-hours, child-labour, low-wage) Indian mills enjoyed — a useful "political economy of regulation" angle.

Q1. The Permanent Settlement of 1793 was introduced by: Recall

(a) Rapid and sustained output growth   (b) Very slow aggregate output growth, with declining foodgrain output per capita   (c) A sharp decline in total output   (d) Uniform growth across all regions

Q5. Amartya Sen's "Entitlement Approach" to famine primarily argues that: Application
(a) Famines are always caused by an absolute decline in food supply   (b) Famines can occur due to a collapse in specific groups' ability to command/access food, even without a sharp overall food-supply decline   (c) Railways always prevent famines   (d) Famine Commissions were fully effective

Answer: (b) — most famously applied to the 1943 Bengal Famine.
Q6. The railway "Guarantee System" refers to: Recall
(a) A guarantee of free rail travel for Indians   (b) A guaranteed minimum rate of return (~5%) to private British railway companies, funded by Indian revenues if shortfalls occurred   (c) A government guarantee of employment for railway workers   (d) Free import of Indian-made railway equipment

Answer: (b).
Q7. Which historian is most associated with a skeptical/critical view of the "de-industrialization" thesis? Recall
(a) Bipan Chandra   (b) Amiya Bagchi   (c) Morris D. Morris   (d) Dadabhai Naoroji

Answer: (c) — Morris questioned the reliability of occupational census data underlying the de-industrialization claim.
Q8. India's modern cotton textile industry (Bombay) was pioneered predominantly by: Application
(a) British managing agencies exclusively   (b) Indian (Parsi and Gujarati) capital   (c) The colonial government directly   (d) Marwari capital exclusively

Answer: (b) — in contrast to Calcutta's jute industry, which remained predominantly under British managing agency control.
Q9. "Discriminating Protection" in interwar India refers to: Conceptual
(a) A blanket tariff wall around all Indian industries   (b) Selective tariff protection granted to specific industries after Tariff Board inquiry, from 1923 onward   (c) A policy banning all imports   (d) Free trade with no tariffs at all

Answer: (b) — following the Indian Fiscal Commission of 1921-22.
Q10. The "jobber" (or sardar) in colonial industrial labour relations was: Conceptual
(a) A government labour inspector   (b) A labour intermediary who recruited and supervised workers on behalf of mill owners, often with considerable informal power   (c) A trade union leader   (d) A British factory manager

Answer: (b) — especially prominent in Bombay's textile mills.
Q11. The All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) was founded in: Recall
(a) 1885   (b) 1907   (c) 1920   (d) 1947

Answer: (c) — the first national-level trade union federation in India.
Q12. Which of the following is a reason often cited for the weak "linkage effects" of railways on Indian industry? Application
(a) Railways were never built in India   (b) Railway equipment (locomotives, rails) was overwhelmingly imported from Britain rather than manufactured domestically   (c) Railways only carried passengers, not goods   (d) Railways were built entirely with Indian capital

Answer: (b) — the potential domestic capital-goods/engineering stimulus was largely captured by British industry instead.
