Introduction to Business Law

Definition, scope, importance · Elements of a contract · Essentials of a valid contract · Types of contracts · Performance obligations · Breach & remedies · Product liability & consumer protection · Business torts · Employment law

Business Law — Definition, Scope, Importance
Business Law = the body of rules (statutory + case law) that governs commercial transactions, business conduct, and relationships between businesses, individuals, and government.
  • Scope
    • Contracts, sale of goods, negotiable instruments
    • Company/partnership law, consumer protection, employment law
    • Torts related to business, intellectual property
  • Importance / role of law in business
    • Provides certainty & predictability to transactions
    • Protects rights of parties (buyers, sellers, employees, consumers)
    • Provides remedies when agreements are broken
    • Maintains fair competition & ethical conduct
    • Builds trust needed for trade & economic growth
Elements of a Contract
Contract = a legally enforceable agreement between two or more parties creating obligations that the law will enforce.
  • Offer & Acceptance
    • Offer — a definite promise/proposal made by the offeror to be bound on certain terms
    • Acceptance — unqualified, unconditional agreement to the exact terms of the offer, communicated to the offeror ("mirror image rule")
    • A counter-offer cancels the original offer
  • Consideration
    • Something of value exchanged between the parties (money, goods, service, a promise) — the "price" for the promise
    • Must be sufficient (have some value) but need not be adequate (need not be a fair/market value)
    • Past consideration is generally not valid consideration
  • Contractual Capacity
    • Legal ability of a party to enter into a binding contract
    • Persons with limited/no capacity — minors, persons of unsound mind, intoxicated persons (contracts may be void/voidable)
Mnemonic: O-A-C-C — Offer, Acceptance, Consideration, Capacity
Essentials of a Valid Contract
  • Valid offer & valid acceptance
  • Lawful consideration
  • Capacity of parties to contract
  • Free consent (no coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation, mistake)
  • Lawful object/purpose (not illegal, not against public policy)
  • Certainty of terms (not vague/ambiguous)
  • Possibility of performance
  • Intention to create legal relations
  • Compliance with legal formalities (writing/registration, where required)
Types of Contracts
  • By validity
    • Valid — fulfills all essentials
    • Void — no legal effect at all
    • Voidable — enforceable at the option of one party (e.g. due to coercion)
    • Illegal — forbidden by law
    • Unenforceable — valid but cannot be enforced due to a technical defect (e.g. lack of writing)
  • By formation
    • Express contract — terms stated in words (oral/written)
    • Implied contract — terms inferred from conduct of parties
    • Quasi-contract — obligation imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment, even without an actual agreement
  • By performance
    • Executed — fully performed by both parties
    • Executory — yet to be performed (wholly/partly)
    • Unilateral — one party makes a promise in exchange for an act
    • Bilateral — both parties exchange promises
Performance Obligations
  • Actual performance — each party fulfills obligations exactly as agreed
  • Attempted performance/Tender — offering to perform, but the other party refuses to accept
  • Modes of discharge of contract
    • By performance, by agreement/mutual consent, by breach, by impossibility (frustration), by lapse of time, by operation of law
Types of Contract Breaches & Remedies
  • Types of breach
    • Anticipatory breach — a party indicates in advance they will not perform
    • Actual breach — failure to perform when performance is due
    • Minor (partial) breach — a small part of the contract not fulfilled
    • Material (total) breach — a fundamental term not fulfilled, defeating the purpose of the contract
  • Remedies for breach
    • Damages — monetary compensation for loss suffered
      • Compensatory (actual loss), Consequential (indirect/foreseeable loss), Punitive (rare, to punish), Nominal (small symbolic sum), Liquidated (pre-agreed amount in the contract)
    • Specific performance — court order requiring the breaching party to actually perform (used when damages are inadequate, e.g. unique goods/property)
    • Rescission — cancelling the contract, restoring parties to pre-contract position
    • Injunction — court order preventing a party from doing a particular act
    • Restitution — returning benefits received to prevent unjust enrichment
Product Liability & Consumer Protection Laws
Product liability = legal responsibility of manufacturers/sellers for injuries or damages caused by defective products.
  • Bases of product liability
    • Negligence — failure to exercise reasonable care in design/manufacture
    • Warranty — breach of express/implied warranty of quality or fitness
    • Strict liability — liable for defective/unreasonably dangerous products regardless of fault
  • Types of product defects
    • Manufacturing defect, design defect, defect in warning/labelling (failure to warn)
  • Consumer protection laws
    • Protect consumers against unfair trade practices, defective goods, deficient services
    • Provide for consumer rights — right to safety, information, choice, redressal
    • Establish consumer dispute redressal forums/commissions
Business Torts
Tort = a civil wrong (other than breach of contract) that causes harm/loss, for which the injured party can claim damages.
  • Common business torts
    • Fraud/misrepresentation — false statement inducing another to act to their detriment
    • Interference with contractual relations — inducing a party to break a contract with a third party
    • Unfair competition — deceptive trade practices, passing off
    • Defamation — false statement harming another's reputation
    • Trade secret misappropriation
Employment Law
  • Governs the employer–employee relationship — hiring, wages, working conditions, termination
  • Key areas
    • Employment contracts & terms of service
    • Minimum wages & working hours regulation
    • Workplace safety & health standards
    • Anti-discrimination & equal opportunity laws
    • Termination, retrenchment, and severance rules
    • Employee benefits & social security obligations

Sales and Leases

Formation of sales contracts · Leasing of goods · Title & risk of loss · Performance & remedies · Warranties & product liability · Negotiable instruments · Bank–customer relations & EFT

Formation of a Sales Contract
Contract of Sale of Goods = an agreement where the seller transfers or agrees to transfer ownership (property) in goods to the buyer for a price.
  • Sale vs Agreement to Sell
    • Sale — ownership transfers immediately
    • Agreement to sell — ownership transfers at a future date/on fulfilment of conditions
  • Essential elements
    • Two parties, goods as subject matter, transfer of ownership, price (money consideration)
Contracts for Leasing Goods
Lease of goods = a contract where the owner (lessor) transfers the right to possess and use goods to another (lessee) for a period, in return for payment (rent), without transferring ownership.
  • Sale vs Lease
    AspectSaleLease
    OwnershipTransfers to buyerRemains with lessor
    PaymentOne-time pricePeriodic rent
    DurationPermanent transferFixed/limited term
Title and Risk of Loss
  • Title — legal ownership of the goods
    • Passes when parties intend it to pass, per contract terms (e.g. on delivery, on payment, on shipment)
  • Risk of loss — who bears the loss if goods are damaged/destroyed before delivery is complete
    • General rule: risk generally follows the passing of ownership (unless otherwise agreed)
    • In shipment contracts, risk may pass to buyer once goods are handed to carrier
    • In destination contracts, risk passes only upon delivery to the buyer's location
Performance & Remedies (Sales)
  • Seller's duties — deliver goods of the contracted description, quality, and quantity, at the agreed time/place
  • Buyer's duties — accept goods & pay the price as agreed
  • Remedies of seller (buyer's breach)
    • Suit for price, suit for damages, right of lien, stoppage in transit, resale of goods
  • Remedies of buyer (seller's breach)
    • Suit for damages, suit for specific performance, right to reject goods, right to rescind
Warranties & Product Liability
  • Types of warranties
    • Express warranty — explicitly stated by seller (words, description, sample, model)
    • Implied warranty of merchantability — goods are fit for ordinary purposes for which such goods are used
    • Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose — arises when seller knows buyer's specific purpose and buyer relies on seller's skill/judgment
  • Breach of warranty → buyer can claim damages / reject goods (product liability principles from Unit 1 also apply here)
Negotiable Instruments — Introduction
Negotiable Instrument = a written document that guarantees payment of a specific amount of money, either on demand or at a set time, and can be transferred from one person to another.
  • Common types
    • Promissory note, Bill of exchange, Cheque
  • Characteristics
    • Freely transferable
    • Transferee (in due course) gets a good title even if transferor's title was defective
    • Right to sue in one's own name
Negotiability, Negotiation & Holder in Due Course
  • Negotiability — the quality of an instrument that allows it to be transferred so the transferee can sue on it in their own name, and take it free of certain defects if they qualify as a holder in due course
  • Negotiation — the act of transferring an instrument from one person to another so as to make the transferee the holder
    • By delivery — for bearer instruments
    • By endorsement + delivery — for order instruments
  • Holder in Due Course (HDC) — a person who takes a negotiable instrument:
    • For value/consideration
    • Before it was overdue
    • In good faith, without notice of any defect in the title
    Key benefit: An HDC gets a better title than the transferor and takes the instrument free from most defenses.
Liability & Discharge (Negotiable Instruments)
  • Liability of parties
    • Maker/Drawer — primarily liable to pay
    • Acceptor/Drawee — liable once they accept the instrument
    • Endorser — liable to subsequent holders unless endorsement says "without recourse"
  • Discharge of instrument/parties
    • By payment in due course, by cancellation, by release, by allowing drawee more time without consent of other parties
Bank–Customer Relations & Electronic Fund Transfers
  • Bank–customer relationship
    • Primarily a debtor–creditor relationship (bank owes money to depositor)
    • Also involves elements of agency (bank as agent for collection of cheques) and bailment (safe deposit lockers)
    • Bank's duty — honour cheques (if funds available), maintain secrecy of accounts, act on customer's mandate
  • Electronic Fund Transfer (EFT)
    EFT = transfer of money from one bank account to another electronically without physical exchange of cash/cheques.
    • Modes — NEFT, RTGS, IMPS, UPI, wire transfer, ATM/debit card transactions
    • Legal issues — unauthorized transactions, liability allocation between bank & customer, data security/privacy

QUICK RECALL — UNIT 2

  • Sale = ownership transfers now; Agreement to sell = transfers later
  • Risk of loss generally follows passing of ownership/title
  • Warranties: Express, Implied-Merchantability, Implied-Fitness for purpose
  • Holder in Due Course = value + before overdue + good faith, no notice of defect
  • Bank–customer relation = primarily debtor–creditor

Introduction to Business Ethics

Definition & importance · Ethics in the Indian context · Institutionalization of ethics · Benefits of ethical conduct · Stakeholder concerns · CSR & regulatory framework · Ethics in marketing/finance/HR · MNC ethics · Global ethical dilemmas

Definition & Importance of Business Ethics
Business Ethics = the application of moral principles and standards to business behaviour, decisions, and practices — distinguishing right from wrong conduct in a commercial context.
  • Why business ethics matters
    • Builds trust with customers, employees, investors, and society
    • Reduces legal/regulatory risk and reputational damage
    • Improves employee morale & long-term sustainability
    • Supports fair competition and healthy markets
  • Business ethics in the Indian context
    • Rooted in traditional values — Dharma (duty), Karmayoga (ethics of action), trusteeship (Gandhian concept of wealth)
    • Modern context — CSR is legally mandated under Indian company law for qualifying companies
    • Balancing traditional value systems with global business practices
Institutionalization of Business Ethics
Institutionalization of ethics = building ethics formally and permanently into an organization's structure, systems, and culture — rather than leaving it to individual discretion.
  • Mechanisms
    • Code of ethics/conduct — written statement of values & expected behaviour
    • Ethics committees & ethics officers/compliance officers
    • Ethics training programmes for employees
    • Whistleblower policies & hotlines
    • Ethics audits & performance reviews including ethical behaviour
Benefits of Ethical Conduct in Business
  • Enhanced brand reputation & customer loyalty
  • Better employee retention & engagement
  • Reduced legal costs, fines, and litigation risk
  • Easier access to capital (investors prefer ethically-run firms)
  • Long-term sustainability & competitive advantage
  • Stronger relationships with regulators & communities
Ethical Issues & Stakeholder Concerns
Stakeholder = any individual/group affected by, or able to affect, an organization's actions (e.g. employees, customers, shareholders, suppliers, community, government).
  • Common ethical issues
    • Conflicts of interest, bribery & corruption
    • Insider trading, misleading advertising
    • Discrimination, harassment, unsafe working conditions
    • Environmental harm, data privacy violations
  • Stakeholder concerns by group
    • Shareholders — honest reporting, fair returns
    • Employees — fair wages, safety, non-discrimination
    • Customers — safe products, honest marketing, fair pricing
    • Community/society — environmental responsibility, philanthropy
Social Responsibility & Regulatory Framework
  • Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)
    CSR = a company's commitment to conducting business in a way that is economically, socially, and environmentally sustainable, while accounting for stakeholder interests beyond shareholders alone.
    • Carroll's CSR pyramid: Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic responsibilities (bottom to top)
    • In India — CSR spending mandated by law for eligible large companies
  • Environment & business
    • Sustainable/green business practices, pollution control compliance
    • Triple Bottom Line — People, Planet, Profit
Ethics in Marketing, Finance & HR Functions
  • Marketing ethics
    • Truthful advertising, fair pricing, avoiding deceptive/manipulative tactics, respecting consumer privacy
  • Finance/accounting ethics
    • Accurate financial reporting, avoiding fraud/window-dressing, insider trading prevention, fair dealing with investors
  • HR ethics
    • Fair recruitment & promotion, non-discrimination, privacy of employee data, safe working conditions, fair compensation
Ethical Responsibilities of Multinational Corporations (MNCs)
  • Complying with laws of both home & host countries
  • Avoiding exploitation of weaker regulatory environments (e.g. lower safety/labour standards abroad)
  • Respecting local culture while upholding universal human rights standards
  • Fair treatment of local employees and suppliers (avoiding sweatshop conditions)
  • Responsible transfer pricing and tax practices
Ethical Dilemmas Facing Businesses Globally
  • Discrimination — unequal treatment based on gender, race, religion, disability in hiring/pay/promotion
  • Human rights — forced/child labour in supply chains, unsafe working conditions abroad
  • Environmental impact — pollution, resource depletion, climate change contribution
  • Intellectual property — piracy, counterfeiting, patent infringement across borders
Exam tip: These four form a common essay-type question: "Discuss global ethical dilemmas MNCs face" — use this list as your answer skeleton.

  • Carroll's CSR pyramid: Economic → Legal → Ethical → Philanthropic
  • Triple Bottom Line: People, Planet, Profit

The Ethical Decision-Making Process

Philosophical & religious approaches · Moral & legal aspects · Indian philosophical sources (Gita, Arthashastra, Vivekananda) · Individual & organizational factors · Decision-making frameworks · Corporate governance · Whistleblowing · Conflict resolution

Philosophical Approaches to Ethical Decision-Making
  • Consequentialism / Utilitarianism
    • An act is ethical if it produces the greatest good for the greatest number — judged by outcomes/consequences
  • Deontology (Kant)
    • Ethics based on duty & rules, not outcomes — an act is right if it follows a universal moral rule regardless of consequences ("categorical imperative")
  • Virtue Ethics (Aristotle)
    • Focuses on the character of the decision-maker — acting as a person of good character (honesty, courage, fairness) would act
  • Rights-based approach
    • An action is ethical if it respects the fundamental rights of all affected individuals
  • Justice/Fairness approach
    • Ethical decisions treat people equitably and distribute benefits/burdens fairly
Ethics & Religious Approaches; Moral & Legal Aspects
  • Religious approaches to ethics
    • Major religions provide moral codes guiding honesty, compassion, non-harm, and justice in dealings — often the historical basis of secular ethical/legal systems
  • Moral aspects — personal conscience, internalized values, sense of right/wrong beyond legal compulsion
  • Legal aspects — codified minimum standards enforced by the state; note:
    Key distinction: Legal ≠ Ethical. An act can be legal but unethical (e.g. exploiting a legal loophole) and vice versa.
Ethical Aspects in Indian Philosophy
  • Bhagavad Gita
    • Teaches performing one's duty (Nishkama Karma — desireless/detached action) without attachment to results
    • Emphasizes duty (Dharma) over personal gain — a foundation for ethical decision-making at work
  • Kautilya's Arthashastra
    • Ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economics & ethics of governance/administration
    • Discusses ethical conduct of rulers/administrators, welfare of subjects, and pragmatic (artha-focused) yet duty-bound governance
  • Swami Vivekananda on Ethics
    • Emphasized selfless service (seva), strength of character, and spiritual foundation of ethical action
    • Believed true ethics arises from recognizing the divine potential in every individual
  • Vivekananda's Message to the Youth of India
    • Called for self-confidence, courage, and moral strength as the basis for nation-building
    • Stressed education of character alongside intellect
  • Karmyog (Karma Yoga) — Indian Philosophy of Work Ethics
    • Work performed as duty/service, done with sincerity and excellence, without selfish attachment to reward
    • Applied to modern business — encourages integrity, diligence, and purpose-driven work
  • Integral Humanism
    • A holistic philosophy integrating individual, family, society, nation, and universe — advocates balanced material and spiritual development, decentralization, and ethical economic policy rooted in Indian values
Ethical Decision-Making in Organizations
  • Individual factors influencing ethical decisions
    • Personal values, moral development stage, family/cultural upbringing
    • Personal integrity, ego strength, locus of control
  • Organizational factors influencing ethical decisions
    • Organizational culture & leadership's ethical tone ("tone at the top")
    • Reward/incentive systems (do they encourage cutting corners?)
    • Peer influence & group norms
    • Presence/absence of formal codes, policies, and oversight
Ethical Decision-Making Frameworks
  • General steps in an ethical decision-making framework
    • 1. Identify the ethical issue/dilemma
    • 2. Gather relevant facts & identify stakeholders
    • 3. Identify possible alternative courses of action
    • 4. Evaluate alternatives against ethical principles (utilitarian, rights, justice, virtue)
    • 5. Make the decision & take action
    • 6. Reflect on/evaluate the outcome
  • Purpose — improves consistency, transparency, and defensibility of decisions; reduces reliance on ad hoc judgment
Corporate Governance & Its Impact on Ethical Decision-Making
Corporate Governance = the system of rules, practices, and processes by which a company is directed and controlled, balancing the interests of shareholders, management, and other stakeholders.
  • Key elements
    • Independent board of directors, audit committees
    • Transparency & accurate disclosure to shareholders
    • Accountability mechanisms & internal controls
    • Protection of minority shareholder rights
  • Impact on ethics
    • Strong governance reduces fraud, conflicts of interest, and abuse of power
    • Poor governance (e.g. weak boards, related-party favoritism) enables unethical practices
Whistleblowing
Whistleblowing = disclosure by an employee (or other insider) of illegal, unethical, or improper conduct within an organization, to internal authorities or external parties/regulators.
  • Types
    • Internal — reporting within the organization (to management, ethics hotline)
    • External — reporting to outside bodies (regulators, media, law enforcement)
  • Importance — early detection of fraud/misconduct, protects public interest
  • Challenges — fear of retaliation, job loss, social ostracism → need for whistleblower protection policies/laws
Conflict Resolution
  • Common approaches to resolving ethical/business conflicts
    • Negotiation — parties directly discuss to reach mutual agreement
    • Mediation — neutral third party facilitates a resolution (non-binding)
    • Arbitration — neutral third party makes a binding decision
    • Litigation — resolution through the court system (formal, binding, adversarial)
  • Ethical conflict resolution aims to balance fairness to all stakeholders with practical business needs
