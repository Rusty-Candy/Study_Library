INDIAN ECONOMY · MACRO TRENDS

National Income, Population & Occupational Structure

The three foundational macro-trends of the Indian economy: how fast national income has grown and how its composition has shifted; how India's population has grown, aged, and is now transitioning; and why the occupational (workforce) structure still lags the output structure — the classic "structural change puzzle."

Trends in National Income

01 — GROWTH TRAJECTORY

Trends in National Income Growth

India's growth trajectory is conventionally divided into distinct phases, each with a different average growth rate — a frequently tested periodisation.

Exam angle
"Hindu rate of growth" is a term coined by economist Raj Krishna to describe India's low (~3.5%) average growth during 1950-80, often attributed to excessive state control, the licence-permit raj, and low productivity growth.
02 — SECTORAL COMPOSITION

Sectoral Composition of GVA: The Great Reshuffle

The clearest structural fact about the Indian economy since Independence: the share of agriculture in output has collapsed, while services has become dominant — a pattern that (unusually, by historical standards) largely bypassed a strong manufacturing-led phase.

Table 2.1 — Sectoral share of GVA over time (illustrative, at current/constant prices, approximate)
YearAgriculture & AlliedIndustryServices
1950-51~53%~15%~32%
1990-91~30%~27%~43%
2010-11~18%~31%~51%
2023-24 (constant prices)~15%~31%~55%
Latest snapshot (FY 2023-24, constant prices)
Services ≈ 55% of GVA · Industry ≈ 31% · Agriculture & Allied ≈ 15% of GVA (source: NSO/MoSPI; ICRA estimates). Within industry, manufacturing itself remains under 20% of GVA even after "Make in India."
The "leapfrogging" / skipped-stage debate
Classic development theory (Fisher-Clark, Kuznets) predicts economies move Agriculture → Industry → Services sequentially, with a strong manufacturing-led middle phase (as in East Asian economies). India's industrial share has instead plateaued around 25-31% of GVA for decades — India is often described as having "leapfrogged" into services-led growth without a full industrialisation phase, a pattern debated as both an opportunity (IT/services strength) and a concern (limited mass job creation, since services growth is often skill/capital-intensive).
03 — WHY SERVICES

Structural Change: Why Has Services Grown So Fast?

  • IT & IT-enabled Services (ITeS) boom since the 1990s — leveraging English-language skills and a large technical talent pool
  • Liberalisation (1991) opened services (banking, insurance, telecom) to private/foreign participation
  • Income elasticity of demand for services rises as per capita income grows (Engel's Law extended)
  • Outsourcing/offshoring of business processes by global firms to India
  • Statistical/measurement factors — some argue services growth is partly a measurement artefact (better capture of an already-large informal services sector over time)
Exam angle
Within services, "Financial, Real Estate & Professional Services" and "Trade, Hotels, Transport & Communication" are typically the largest sub-components — worth remembering the standard three-way sectoral classification used in national accounts: Primary (Agri, Mining), Secondary (Manufacturing, Electricity, Construction), Tertiary (Trade, Transport, Finance, Public Admin, etc.).

Part B

Trends in Population

05 — SIZE & GROWTH

Population Size, Growth & the Demographic Transition

Latest figures
India's population is estimated at approximately 146.4 crore (1.464 billion) as of April 2025 (UNFPA 2025 report) — making India the world's most populous country, having overtaken China (≈141.6 crore) around 2023. Annual population growth rate has slowed to roughly 0.89% (down sharply from ~2.3% in the 1970s-80s).
Demographic Transition Theory
A model describing how a country's birth and death rates evolve through economic development, typically in four stages:

06 — AGE STRUCTURE

Age Structure & the Demographic Dividend

India's age structure (2025 estimates)
0-14 years: ≈24%  |  Working-age 15-64 years: ≈68%  |  65+ years: ≈7%. Median age ≈29.8 years (vs ≈40.2 in China) — one of the youngest large populations in the world.
Demographic Dividend
The potential economic growth boost that arises when the working-age population (15-64) grows larger, relative to the young and old dependents, creating a "window" of higher productive capacity and savings potential — provided this workforce is adequately educated, skilled, and employed.
Dependency Ratio
Dependency Ratio = [(Population 0-14) + (Population 65+)] / (Population 15-64) × 100

A falling dependency ratio (as India is experiencing) is the arithmetic signature of the demographic dividend window — fewer dependents per working-age adult, in principle freeing up resources for savings, investment, and growth.

The dividend is not automatic
A "demographic dividend" only materialises if the growing working-age population is employed productively. If job creation lags behind labour force growth, the same demographic trend becomes a "demographic burden" instead — rising youth unemployment/underemployment. This tension is central to current Indian policy debates (education quality, skilling missions, labour-intensive manufacturing).
Exam angle
Distinguish "demographic dividend" (an opportunity, contingent on productive employment) from "demographic transition" (the underlying process of falling birth/death rates that creates the age-structure shift enabling the dividend).

The delayed Census
India's decennial Census, originally due in 2021, has been delayed (initially attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic) — current estimates use projections from the Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, the Sample Registration System (SRS), the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), and UN/UNFPA modelling, pending the next full Census.
Exam angle
Note the key data sources for Indian population statistics — since the Census itself is delayed, questions may ask which body/survey provides a given estimate: Census of India (decennial, Registrar General of India), SRS (annual, for birth/death rates), NFHS (periodic, for health/fertility indicators).
08 — DEBATE

Population: Asset or Liability? The Classic Debate

Table 8.1 — Two views
Population as a "Liability" (Malthusian/pessimistic view)Population as an "Asset" (Optimistic/human-capital view)
Large population strains scarce resources, land, food supply (Malthusian trap)Large population = large market size, larger labour force, more potential innovators (Julian Simon's "Ultimate Resource" view)
Raises the "dependency burden" if age structure is unfavourableA youthful population offers a demographic dividend if skilled and employed
Environmental degradation, pressure on per capita availability of land/waterEconomies of scale in production and provision of infrastructure/services

Modern development economics increasingly treats population less as a fixed "problem" and more as a function of the quality of human capital (education, health, skills) — the same population size can be either a dividend or a burden depending on these investments.

Part C

Occupational Structure

09 — MEANING

Meaning & the Historical Trend

Occupational Structure
The distribution of a country's working population (workforce) across the three broad sectors — Primary (agriculture & allied), Secondary (industry/manufacturing), and Tertiary (services) — as distinct from the sectoral distribution of output/GVA covered in Part A.
Table 9.1 — Long-run shift in India's workforce distribution (illustrative, %)
YearAgricultureIndustryServices
1951~72%~11%~17%
1987-88~66%~13%~21%
2011-12~49%~24%~27%
2023-24 (PLFS)~46%~25%~29%
Exam angle
The historical decline of agriculture's workforce share — from ~66% in 1987-88 to ~46% in 2023-24 — is real but has been far slower than the decline in agriculture's output (GVA) share over the same period (from ~30% to ~15%). This gap is the essence of the "structural transformation puzzle" (Section 12).
10 — CURRENT DATA

Current Workforce Distribution (PLFS 2023-24)

Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2023-24 — headline numbers
Agriculture ≈ 46.1% of workers (rising slightly from 45.5% in 2021-22 and 45.8% in 2022-23)  |  Labour Force Participation Rate (LFPR) rose to 45.1%  |  Worker Population Ratio (WPR) rose to 43.7%  |  Unemployment Rate stagnant at ~3.2%. India's total workforce is estimated at roughly 611 million (2023-24), with about 42 million jobs added over the year — nearly half of them in agriculture itself.

Exam angle
This GVA-share vs workforce-share mismatch is exactly why per-worker income (productivity) in agriculture is so much lower than in industry/services — a fact frequently tested via numbers or via the underlying logic (excess/"disguised" employment in agriculture; see Section 11).
11 — WHY AGRICULTURE PERSISTS

Why Does Agriculture Still Dominate the Workforce?

  • Disguised unemployment — a large number of workers on farms whose Marginal Product of Labour is at or near zero; removing them would not reduce output (classic feature of an agrarian, labour-surplus economy — see Lewis's Dual-Sector Model)
  • Slow pace of non-farm job creation — manufacturing (which should absorb surplus agricultural labour, per the classic development path) has not grown fast enough in employment-intensive segments
  • Land as a safety net / fallback occupation — informal, self-employment and unpaid family labour on small landholdings absorb workers when formal opportunities are scarce, especially during downturns
  • Low education/skill levels among rural workers limiting mobility into higher-productivity non-farm jobs
  • Seasonal reverse migration — evidence (post-pandemic, and again in recent PLFS rounds) of workers moving back into agriculture during economic stress, partly explaining the slight recent uptick in agriculture's employment share (45.5% → 46.1%) even as its output share keeps falling
Lewis Dual-Sector Model (brief)
W. Arthur Lewis's model describes economic development as the gradual transfer of "surplus labour" from a low-productivity traditional (agricultural) sector to a high-productivity modern (industrial/urban) sector, drawn by a wage differential — until the surplus is exhausted (the "Lewis turning point").
12 — THE PUZZLE

Structural Transformation: A Puzzle, Not a Textbook Path

India's atypical transformation
Standard development theory (Fisher-Clark-Kuznets, Lewis) predicts workforce should move Agriculture → Industry → Services broadly in step with output. India instead saw output shift rapidly to Services while workforce remained disproportionately stuck in Agriculture, with Industry's employment share never rising as much as theory would predict (limited "labour-intensive manufacturing" phase).
Table 12.1 — India vs the "classic" development path
Classic path (e.g., East Asian "Tigers")India's actual path
SequenceAgriculture → labour-intensive manufacturing (absorbs surplus labour) → servicesAgriculture → skill/capital-intensive services (bypassing a strong labour-absorbing manufacturing phase)
Employment generationManufacturing absorbs large numbers of low-skilled workersServices growth (IT, finance) is often capital/skill-intensive, absorbing fewer low-skilled workers per unit of output
ConsequenceFaster, broader-based poverty reduction via employment shiftLarge "residual" workforce remains trapped in low-productivity agriculture/informal sector — a key structural challenge

Policy implications frequently discussed

  • Need to boost labour-intensive manufacturing (textiles, leather, food processing) to absorb agricultural surplus labour
  • Skilling missions (e.g., Skill India) to enable workforce mobility into higher-productivity sectors
  • Addressing informality — the vast majority of India's workforce remains in informal employment, with limited social security
  • Raising agricultural productivity itself (irrigation, technology, market reforms) even as the sector's workforce share gradually falls
Exam angle
This "output-employment elasticity mismatch" — the idea that GDP/GVA growth in India has often been less employment-intensive than historically expected — is one of the most important conceptual threads connecting national income trends (Part A), population/demographic dividend (Part B), and occupational structure (Part C) into a single coherent narrative. Expect integrated questions linking all three.

Q1. The term "Hindu rate of growth" refers to India's average growth rate during: Recall
(a) 1991-2000   (b) 1950-1980   (c) 2000-2010   (d) 2010-2020

Answer: (b) — coined by economist Raj Krishna to describe the low (~3.5%) average growth of that period.
Q2. Which sector currently contributes the largest share of India's GVA? Recall
(a) Agriculture   (b) Industry   (c) Services   (d) Mining

Answer: (c) — services account for roughly 55% of GVA, the majority.
Q3. India's occupational structure differs from its output structure mainly because: Conceptual
(a) Industry employs the most workers   (b) A disproportionately large share of the workforce remains in low-productivity agriculture despite its shrinking output share   (c) Services employ the majority of workers   (d) There is no difference between the two structures

Answer: (b) — agriculture employs ~46% of workers but generates only ~15% of GVA.
Q4. "Disguised unemployment" in Indian agriculture refers to: Conceptual
(a) Workers who are registered as unemployed   (b) Workers whose marginal product is near zero, so their removal would not reduce total output   (c) Seasonal unemployment during off-harvest months   (d) Unemployment among the urban educated

Answer: (b) — a central feature of the Lewis Dual-Sector Model's "surplus labour."
Q5. India's Total Fertility Rate (TFR) as of recent estimates is: Current Affairs
(a) Above the replacement level of 2.1   (b) Below the replacement level, at approximately 1.9–2.0   (c) Exactly at 3.0   (d) Rising rapidly

Answer: (b) — a major demographic shift from earlier decades of high fertility.
Q6. The "demographic dividend" refers to: Recall
(a) Guaranteed economic growth from any population increase   (b) The growth potential from a rising working-age share of the population, realised only if that workforce is productively employed   (c) Cash transfers to young workers   (d) A tax on population growth

Answer: (b).
Q7. According to the Demographic Transition model, "Stage 2" is characterised by: Conceptual
(a) Both high birth and death rates   (b) High birth rate but rapidly falling death rate, causing a population explosion   (c) Low birth and death rates   (d) Falling birth rate with stable death rate

Answer: (b).
Q8. India's national accounts currently measure GDP/GVA using which base year? Recall
(a) 2004-05   (b) 2011-12   (c) 2018-19   (d) 1999-2000

Answer: (b).
Q9. India's decennial Census, originally due in 2021, has been: Current Affairs
(a) Completed on schedule   (b) Delayed, with current population figures relying on projections/surveys (SRS, NFHS, UNFPA)   (c) Cancelled permanently   (d) Replaced entirely by PLFS

Answer: (b).
Q10. Which of the following best describes India's structural transformation, compared to the "classic" East Asian development path? Application
(a) India followed an identical Agriculture→Manufacturing→Services employment shift   (b) India's output shifted rapidly to services, while a comparably strong labour-absorbing manufacturing phase was largely bypassed   (c) India's agriculture sector's output share increased over time   (d) India's workforce moved entirely into industry

Answer: (b) — the central "structural transformation puzzle" of the Indian economy.
Q11. The Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) is conducted by: Recall
(a) RBI   (b) Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI/NSO)   (c) NITI Aayog   (d) Ministry of Labour directly, without any survey agency

Answer: (b) — conducted since 2017-18, on a July-June survey cycle.
Q12. A rising Gross Domestic Savings Rate primarily reflects: Conceptual
(a) Rising government expenditure   (b) A rising share of national income being saved rather than consumed, by households, firms, and government combined   (c) Falling national income   (d) Falling foreign investment

Answer: (b) — GDS Rate = GDS/GDP, aggregated across the household, private corporate, and public sectors.
