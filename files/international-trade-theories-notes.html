International Trade Theories: From Smith to New Trade Theory

Why do nations trade at all? From Adam Smith's absolute advantage and Ricardo's comparative advantage, through the Heckscher-Ohlin factor-proportions model and its associated theorems, to Krugman's New Trade Theory explaining trade between similar economies — the full evolution of trade theory.

01 — FOUNDATION

Why Do Nations Trade?

International trade theory asks a deceptively simple question: why should countries specialise and exchange goods, rather than each producing everything it needs domestically? The answer has evolved across four broad waves of theory.

Table 1.1 — The evolution of trade theory
TheoryOriginatorCore basis for trade
Absolute AdvantageAdam Smith (1776)Differences in absolute labour productivity
Comparative AdvantageDavid Ricardo (1817)Differences in relative labour productivity (opportunity cost)
Factor Proportions (H-O)Eli Heckscher & Bertil Ohlin (1919, 1933)Differences in relative factor endowments (capital vs labour abundance)
New Trade TheoryPaul Krugman et al. (1979-80s)Economies of scale & product differentiation — explains trade even between similar countries
02 — CLASSICAL THEORY I

Adam Smith: The Theory of Absolute Advantage

Absolute Advantage
A country has an absolute advantage in producing a good if it can produce more output per unit of input (or use fewer resources per unit of output) than another country. Smith argued each country should specialise in producing the good(s) in which it has an absolute advantage, and trade for the rest — mirroring his broader argument for the division of labour, applied to nations.
Table 2.1 — Illustrative example (units of output per labour-hour)
CountryWheatCloth
Country A62
Country B15

Here, Country A has an absolute advantage in Wheat (6 > 1); Country B has an absolute advantage in Cloth (5 > 2). Smith's theory predicts A specialises in Wheat, B in Cloth, and both trade to mutual benefit.

The gap Smith left open
Absolute advantage theory cannot explain what happens if one country is absolutely more efficient at producing everything — Smith's framework offers no reason for such a country to trade at all, since it has nothing to "gain" in absolute terms from a less efficient partner. This gap was resolved by Ricardo (Section 3).
Exam angle
A very common conceptual question: "Absolute advantage is a special/limiting case of comparative advantage" is True — Ricardo's theory is the more general framework, of which Smith's absolute advantage is a special case where relative efficiencies happen to align with absolute ones.
03 — CLASSICAL THEORY II

Ricardo: The Theory of Comparative Advantage

Comparative Advantage
A country has a comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce it at a lower opportunity cost (relative to its other production alternative) than another country — even if it is absolutely less efficient at producing everything. Mutually beneficial trade is possible whenever relative (opportunity) costs of production differ between countries, regardless of absolute efficiency levels.
Table 3.1 — Ricardo's classic numerical example (labour hours needed per unit)
CountryWine (hours/unit)Cloth (hours/unit)Opportunity cost of Wine (in Cloth)
Portugal809080/90 = 0.89 units of cloth
England120100120/100 = 1.2 units of cloth

Portugal is absolutely more efficient at producing both goods (needs fewer hours for each). But Portugal's opportunity cost of Wine (0.89 cloth) is lower than England's (1.2 cloth) — so Portugal has a comparative advantage in Wine; England, correspondingly, has a comparative advantage in Cloth (lower opportunity cost of cloth = 100/120 = 0.83 wine, vs Portugal's 90/80 = 1.125 wine). Both gain by specialising accordingly and trading.

Exam angle
Ricardo's model rests on the Labour Theory of Value — assuming labour is the only factor of production and productivity differences (technology) alone drive trade patterns. This single-factor assumption is exactly what later theories (Heckscher-Ohlin) relaxed by introducing multiple factors of production.
04 — GAINS FROM TRADE

Gains from Trade & the Terms of Trade Range

Range of mutually beneficial Terms of Trade (using the Ricardo example)
0.89 (Portugal's domestic opportunity cost of wine) < International price ratio (Wine/Cloth) < 1.2 (England's domestic opportunity cost of wine)

As long as the international exchange ratio (terms of trade) settles somewhere between the two countries' domestic opportunity cost ratios, both countries gain from trade relative to producing everything domestically — each gets to consume outside its own PPF. The exact terms of trade within this range (and hence how the gains are split) depends on relative demand strength between the two countries (reciprocal demand, per J.S. Mill's later refinement).

Exam angle
A frequently tested numerical/conceptual point: if the international terms of trade equal exactly one country's domestic opportunity cost ratio, that country gains nothing from trade (it's indifferent between trading and not), while the other country captures the entire gain from trade — a useful edge case to remember.
05 — CRITIQUE

Limitations of the Classical (Smith-Ricardo) Theories

  • Single factor of production (labour only) — ignores capital, land, and other resources entirely
  • Constant costs / linear PPF — assumes constant opportunity costs (a straight-line PPF), unrealistic given diminishing returns in most real production
  • Doesn't explain the source of productivity differences — simply assumes technology/productivity differences exist, without explaining why they arise between countries
  • No transport costs, perfect factor immobility between countries but perfect mobility within — simplifying assumptions that limit real-world applicability
  • Assumes full employment and no unemployment effects of trade/specialisation
  • Two-country, two-good framework — a simplification relaxed in more advanced extensions

The Heckscher-Ohlin model (Section 6) directly addresses the biggest gap — the single-factor assumption — by introducing multiple factors of production and asking what determines comparative advantage in the first place.

06 — NEOCLASSICAL THEORY

Heckscher-Ohlin Model: The Factor Proportions Theory

Core Idea
Unlike Ricardo (who assumed away the source of comparative advantage), Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) explains comparative advantage as arising from differences in countries' relative factor endowments (capital vs labour abundance) combined with differences in goods' relative factor intensities (capital-intensive vs labour-intensive production).

Key assumptions

  • Two countries, two goods, two factors of production (capital K, labour L) — the "2×2×2" model
  • Identical technology (production functions) across countries
  • Identical, homothetic consumer preferences across countries
  • Perfect competition in both goods and factor markets
  • No factor mobility between countries; perfect factor mobility within a country
  • No transport costs or trade barriers
The Heckscher-Ohlin (H-O) Theorem
A country will export the good that intensively uses its relatively abundant factor, and import the good that intensively uses its relatively scarce factor.

Example: a capital-abundant country (like the US, relative to its trading partners) will export capital-intensive goods (machinery) and import labour-intensive goods (textiles); a labour-abundant country (like India or Bangladesh) will do the reverse.

07 — RELATED THEOREMS

Factor Price Equalization Theorem

Factor Price Equalization (FPE) Theorem (Samuelson's extension of H-O)
Free trade in goods, under H-O assumptions, will tend to equalise the relative and absolute returns to factors of production (wages and returns to capital) across trading countries — even without any actual movement of the factors (labour or capital) themselves across borders.

Intuition: trade in goods acts as an indirect substitute for factor mobility — a labour-abundant country exporting labour-intensive goods effectively "exports" its abundant labour embodied in those goods, raising domestic demand for labour (and wages) until, in the theoretical limit, wage rates converge across countries.

A strong, rarely-observed theoretical result
In reality, wages and capital returns are far from equalised internationally — reflecting the theorem's strict, unrealistic assumptions (identical technology, no trade barriers/transport costs, perfect competition) rarely holding in practice. It remains an important theoretical benchmark rather than an empirical prediction.
08 — RELATED THEOREMS

Stolper-Samuelson & Rybczynski Theorems

Stolper-Samuelson Theorem
An increase in the relative price of a good will increase the real return to the factor used intensively in producing that good, and decrease the real return to the other factor — meaning trade liberalisation has clear winners and losers among factor owners within each country.

Example: if a labour-abundant country opens to trade and the relative price of its labour-intensive export good rises, real wages rise while the real return to capital falls — even though the country as a whole gains from trade in aggregate.

Rybczynski Theorem
At constant relative goods prices, an increase in the endowment of one factor (say, capital) will lead to a more than proportionate increase in the output of the good that uses that factor intensively, and an absolute decrease in the output of the other good.
Exam angle
The Stolper-Samuelson Theorem is the theoretical backbone of debates about trade and inequality — it formally shows why, even when a country gains overall from trade, owners of the scarce factor lose in real terms, providing an economic rationale for the political economy of protectionism (e.g., capital owners in a labour-abundant developing country opposing trade liberalisation).
09 — THE EMPIRICAL CHALLENGE

The Leontief Paradox

Leontief Paradox (1953)
Wassily Leontief empirically tested the H-O theorem using US trade data and found the opposite of what H-O predicted: despite the US being the most capital-abundant country in the world at the time, its exports were found to be relatively more labour-intensive than its imports — directly contradicting the H-O theorem's prediction.

Proposed explanations for the paradox

  • Human capital augmentation — US labour should be counted as effectively more "capital-intensive" once skill/education (human capital) embodied in workers is properly accounted for, not just physical capital
  • Factor-intensity reversals — the same good might be produced capital-intensively in one country and labour-intensively in another, violating a key H-O assumption of identical technology/production functions
  • Trade barriers & tariff structure — US tariff policy at the time may have protected labour-intensive domestic industries, distorting the trade pattern the theorem would otherwise predict
  • Natural resources as a third factor — some US exports were natural-resource-intensive, a factor the simple 2-factor (K, L) model ignores
Exam angle
The Leontief Paradox is one of the most important named empirical results in trade theory — always tied to Wassily Leontief, 1953, using US data — and its resolution (particularly the human capital argument) is frequently tested as a follow-up.
10 — MODERN THEORY

New Trade Theory (Krugman)

The Puzzle New Trade Theory Solves
Classical and H-O theories predict trade should occur between dissimilar countries (different productivities or factor endowments) and should be inter-industry (exchanging entirely different types of goods). But in reality, a huge share of world trade is intra-industry (e.g., Germany exports cars to France and imports different car models from France) and occurs predominantly between similar, developed economies — a pattern classical/H-O theory cannot explain.

Krugman's key building blocks

  • Economies of scale (increasing returns) — average costs fall as a firm's output rises, giving large firms/countries a cost advantage independent of factor endowments or technology differences
  • Monopolistic competition — many firms producing differentiated varieties of a similar product (e.g., different car brands/models), giving consumers a "love of variety"
  • Intra-industry trade emerges naturally: even identical countries benefit from trading different varieties of the same broad product category, since consumers value variety and firms benefit from larger combined markets to exploit scale economies
Measuring Intra-Industry Trade — Grubel-Lloyd Index
GL = 1 − |X − M| / (X + M)   (X = exports, M = imports, of a given industry)
GL close to 1 → high intra-industry trade; GL close to 0 → trade is purely inter-industry
Exam angle
New Trade Theory does not replace H-O — it complements it: H-O still explains inter-industry trade (why a labour-abundant country exports textiles and a capital-abundant one exports machinery), while New Trade Theory explains the large volume of intra-industry trade between similar, developed economies. Real-world trade combines both patterns.
11 — OTHER MODERN THEORIES

Other Modern Trade Theories (Brief)

Table 11.1 — Additional named theories worth recognising
TheoryOriginatorCore idea
Product Life Cycle TheoryRaymond Vernon (1966)A product moves through stages (innovation → maturity → standardisation), with production location shifting over time from the innovating (developed) country to lower-cost developing countries as the product/technology matures and standardises
Linder HypothesisStaffan Linder (1961)Countries with similar per capita income levels (and hence similar demand patterns/tastes) trade more manufactured goods with each other than the factor-proportions theory would predict — demand-side explanation for trade between similar, developed economies
Porter's Diamond / National Competitive AdvantageMichael Porter (1990)A country's competitiveness in an industry depends on four interrelated factors: factor conditions, demand conditions, related/supporting industries, and firm strategy/structure/rivalry — a more dynamic, firm/industry-level view of trade competitiveness beyond simple factor endowments
Gravity Model of TradeJan Tinbergen (1962), widely used sinceBilateral trade volume between two countries is predicted to be proportional to the product of their economic sizes (GDP) and inversely related to the distance between them — an empirically robust, widely-used applied trade model

Practice MCQs

Q1. Adam Smith's theory of trade is based on: Recall
(a) Relative factor endowments   (b) Absolute differences in labour productivity   (c) Economies of scale   (d) Consumer preference for variety

Answer: (b).
Q2. Ricardo's key contribution over Smith was to show that mutually beneficial trade is possible: Conceptual
(a) Only when absolute advantage exists   (b) Even when one country is absolutely more efficient at producing everything, as long as opportunity costs differ   (c) Only under a common currency   (d) Only between countries of similar size

Answer: (b).
Q3. The Heckscher-Ohlin theorem predicts that a country will export: Recall
(a) The good that uses its scarce factor intensively   (b) The good that uses its abundant factor intensively   (c) Only agricultural goods   (d) Whatever good has the lowest absolute cost

Answer: (b).
Q4. The Factor Price Equalization Theorem argues that free trade in goods will: Application
(a) Widen wage gaps between trading countries permanently   (b) Tend to equalise factor returns (wages, capital returns) across countries, even without factor mobility   (c) Have no effect on factor prices   (d) Only apply to capital, not labour

Answer: (b).
Q5. The Stolper-Samuelson Theorem implies that trade liberalisation in a labour-abundant country will tend to: Application
(a) Reduce real wages and raise real returns to capital   (b) Raise real wages (the abundant factor) and reduce real returns to capital (the scarce factor)   (c) Leave both factor returns unchanged   (d) Only affect government revenue

Answer: (b).
Q6. The Leontief Paradox refers to the empirical finding that: Recall
(a) US imports were more capital-intensive than predicted, confirming H-O   (b) US exports were found to be relatively more labour-intensive than its imports, contradicting the H-O prediction for a capital-abundant country   (c) The US exported no goods at all   (d) Trade between the US and Europe was zero

Answer: (b) — Wassily Leontief, 1953.
Q7. New Trade Theory (Krugman) primarily explains: Conceptual
(a) Inter-industry trade between dissimilar countries   (b) Intra-industry trade between similar countries, via economies of scale and product differentiation   (c) Why comparative advantage doesn't exist   (d) Trade only in agricultural commodities

Answer: (b).
Q8. A Grubel-Lloyd Index value close to 1 indicates: Numerical
(a) Purely inter-industry trade   (b) High intra-industry trade   (c) No trade at all   (d) A trade deficit

Answer: (b) — GL = 1 − |X−M|/(X+M); a small gap between exports and imports in the same industry implies high intra-industry trade.
Q9. The Linder Hypothesis suggests that trade in manufactured goods is greatest between countries with: Recall
(a) Very different factor endowments   (b) Similar per capita income levels and demand patterns   (c) No diplomatic relations   (d) Identical population sizes only

Answer: (b).
Q10. According to Vernon's Product Life Cycle theory, as a product matures and becomes standardised, production tends to shift to: Application
(a) The original innovating country permanently   (b) Lower-cost, typically developing, countries   (c) No country — production stops   (d) Only countries with the most R&D spending

Answer: (b).
Q11. Ricardo's model of comparative advantage rests critically on the assumption of: Conceptual
(a) Multiple factors of production   (b) A single factor of production (labour) and the labour theory of value   (c) Increasing returns to scale   (d) Imperfect competition

Answer: (b).
Q12. The Rybczynski Theorem states that, at constant goods prices, an increase in a country's capital endowment will: Application
(a) Increase output of both goods proportionately   (b) Increase the capital-intensive good's output more than proportionately, and decrease the labour-intensive good's output absolutely   (c) Have no effect on output   (d) Decrease the capital-intensive good's output

Answer: (b).
