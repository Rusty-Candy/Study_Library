02 — PERFECT COMPETITION

Assumptions of Perfect Competition

  • Large number of buyers and sellers — each too small to influence market price
  • Homogeneous product — perfect substitutes, no branding
  • Free entry and exit — no barriers, firms can enter/exit in the long run
  • Perfect knowledge — of prices and market conditions, by both buyers and sellers
  • Perfect mobility of factors of production
  • No transport costs, no government intervention
  • Firms are price-takers — each faces a perfectly elastic (horizontal) demand curve at the ruling market price
Firm's demand/revenue curve
P = AR = MR  (a single horizontal line at market price P*)
Exam angle
Perfect competition is a theoretical benchmark, almost never observed exactly in reality — its value lies in providing the efficiency yardstick against which other market structures are judged (see Part B).
03 — SHORT RUN

Perfect Competition: Short-Run Equilibrium of the Firm

Equilibrium condition
MR = MC, with MC cutting MR (=P) from below

In the short run, at least one factor (plant size) is fixed, so a firm may earn supernormal profit, normal profit, or even a loss — yet still be in equilibrium as long as MR = MC holds.

The Shutdown Point & Short-Run Supply Curve

Shutdown Point
The point where P = minimum AVC. If price falls below minimum AVC, the firm cannot even cover its variable costs and should shut down immediately (rather than keep producing and losing more than TFC).
Table 3.1 — Firm's decision rule by price range
Price rangeDecisionOutcome
P > min ACProduce where MR=MCSupernormal profit
P = min ACProduce where MR=MCNormal profit (break-even point)
min AVC < P < min ACProduce where MR=MCLoss, but less than TFC — continue producing (covers part of fixed cost)
P = min AVCIndifferent (shutdown point)Loss exactly equal to TFC
P < min AVCShut downProducing would increase losses beyond TFC
Short-run supply curve of the firm
= the portion of the MC curve above (and including) the minimum point of AVC
04 — LONG RUN

Perfect Competition: Long-Run Equilibrium

In the long run, entry and exit eliminate any economic profit or loss: firms enter when profits are positive (shifting supply right, price falls) and exit when losses exist (shifting supply left, price rises) — until only normal profit remains.

Long-run equilibrium condition
P = AR = MR = AC = MC  (at the minimum point of the AC curve)
Exam angle
The tangency-at-minimum condition (P = AC at its minimum) is unique to perfect competition's long run. This is the benchmark of productive efficiency (firms produce at minimum possible average cost) — contrast this with monopolistic competition's "excess capacity" (Section 8), where the tangency occurs left of AC's minimum.
05 — MONOPOLY

Monopoly: Meaning & Sources of Market Power

Monopoly
A market structure with a single seller of a product with no close substitutes, facing the entire (downward-sloping) market demand curve, and protected by barriers to entry.

Sources / Barriers to Entry

  • Legal barriers — patents, copyrights, licences, government franchise
  • Natural monopoly — extreme economies of scale (e.g., utilities: water, electricity grids)
  • Control over key/scarce raw material inputs
  • High capital/technology requirements
  • Network effects and brand loyalty (modern digital markets)

AR–MR relationship under Monopoly

Since the monopolist's AR curve is the downward-sloping market demand curve, MR lies below AR at every output level (except the first unit), and MR can even become negative.

MR–elasticity relationship
MR = P (1 − 1/e)    where e = price elasticity of demand
If e > 1: MR > 0  |  e = 1: MR = 0  |  e < 1: MR < 0

Consequence: a profit-maximising monopolist never operates on the inelastic portion of its demand curve (e < 1), since MR would be negative there and MC is always positive.

06 — MONOPOLY EQUILIBRIUM

Monopoly: Equilibrium & Deadweight Loss

Equilibrium condition
MR = MC  (MC cutting MR from below) — price is then read off the AR (demand) curve at that quantity, P > MC

Key features of monopoly equilibrium

  • Price exceeds Marginal Cost: P > MC (allocative inefficiency)
  • Output is restricted below the competitive/socially optimal level
  • Supernormal profit can persist into the long run (barriers block entry)
  • No unique supply curve exists for a monopolist — the same price can be associated with different quantities depending on the shape of demand (unlike perfect competition, where supply = MC curve above AVC)
Exam angle
"Monopoly has no supply curve" is a classic true/false statement — true because output under monopoly depends on both MC and the elasticity/shape of the demand curve it faces, so price and quantity are not uniquely related the way they are under a competitive firm's MC-based supply curve.
07 — PRICE DISCRIMINATION

Price Discrimination

Price Discrimination
Charging different prices to different buyers (or for different units) for the same good, where the price differences are not justified by cost differences.

Necessary conditions

  • Seller must have some degree of monopoly power
  • Markets/buyers must be separable (segmentable)
  • No resale/arbitrage possible between the segmented markets
  • Different segments must have different price elasticities of demand
Table 7.1 — Degrees of Price Discrimination (Pigou's classification)
DegreeMechanismConsumer Surplus capturedReal-world example
First-degree (perfect)Each unit sold at the maximum price the buyer is willing to payEntire consumer surplus extracted by the sellerPersonalised/dynamic pricing, bargaining, auctions
Second-degreePrice varies by block/quantity purchased (block pricing)Partial — some surplus remains with buyerElectricity/water tariff slabs, bulk discounts
Third-degreeDifferent prices in different markets/segments based on elasticityPartial — varies by segmentStudent/senior discounts, export vs domestic pricing, peak/off-peak fares

Third-Degree Price Discrimination — Equilibrium Condition

Profit-maximising rule
MR1 = MR2 = MC  (aggregate marginal cost, split so marginal revenue is equalised across both markets)
Price is lower in the market with more elastic demand, higher where demand is less elastic.
Exam angle
Remember the inverse rule: Price is inversely related to elasticity across segments under third-degree discrimination — a very common numerical/conceptual MCQ (e.g., "which market gets the higher price: the one with e=1.5 or e=3?" → e=1.5, the less elastic one).
08 — MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION

Monopolistic Competition (Chamberlin, 1933)

A hybrid structure — many sellers (like perfect competition) but a differentiated product (like monopoly), giving each firm limited market power.

Assumptions

  • Large number of sellers, each with a small market share
  • Product differentiation — via branding, quality, packaging, location, service
  • Free entry and exit in the long run
  • Firms incur selling costs (advertising) in addition to production costs
  • Downward-sloping but relatively elastic demand curve for each firm (due to close, though imperfect, substitutes)

Short-Run Equilibrium

Identical in form to monopoly's short-run: MR = MC, and the firm can earn supernormal profit, normal profit, or a loss depending on where AR sits relative to AC.

Long-Run Equilibrium — the Tangency Solution

Long-run condition
MR = MC  AND  AR is tangent to AC (so AR = AC at that point)  ⇒  zero economic (supernormal) profit

Excess Capacity Theorem

Excess Capacity
Because the tangency point occurs where AC is still falling (left of its minimum), the firm under monopolistic competition produces less than the output that would minimise average cost — it operates with "excess" (unused) capacity relative to the perfectly competitive ideal, even though it earns zero economic profit.

Trade-off: consumers get more product variety (differentiation) at the cost of firms not operating at minimum efficient scale.

Exam angle
Compare directly with Perfect Competition's long run (Section 4): both give zero economic profit, but PC achieves productive efficiency (P=min AC) while monopolistic competition does not (tangency left of min AC) — this contrast is a favourite CUET PG comparison question.
09 — DUOPOLY

Duopoly Models

Duopoly — exactly two sellers — is the simplest case of strategic interdependence, and the launchpad for all oligopoly theory. Four classical models are commonly tested.

1. Cournot Model (Quantity Competition, 1838)

Key assumption
Each duopolist chooses its output quantity to maximise profit, assuming the rival's output stays fixed at its current level (a naive/static conjecture).

Each firm has a reaction curve (or reaction function) showing its profit-maximising output as a function of the rival's output. Equilibrium occurs where the two reaction curves intersect — neither firm wants to unilaterally change output, given the other's choice.

Standard result (identical firms, linear demand, zero cost — textbook case)
Each firm produces 1/3 of the competitive output; combined output = 2/3 of competitive output; price lies between the monopoly and competitive price.

2. Bertrand Model (Price Competition, 1883)

Key assumption
Each duopolist chooses price (not quantity), assuming the rival's price stays fixed. With homogeneous products, this triggers aggressive undercutting.

Bertrand Paradox: even with just two firms, price competition drives price all the way down to marginal cost (P = MC) — the same outcome as perfect competition — because each firm has an incentive to undercut the rival as long as price exceeds MC.

3. Stackelberg Model (Leader–Follower, 1934)

Key assumption
One firm (the leader) moves first and sets its output, anticipating how the follower will react (using the follower's Cournot reaction curve); the follower then simply best-responds.

The leader earns higher profit than under simultaneous Cournot competition (first-mover advantage) — it produces more, and the follower produces correspondingly less along its reaction curve.

4. Edgeworth Model (1897)

Key assumption
Like Bertrand, firms compete on price, but each faces a capacity constraint — it cannot supply the entire market alone. This prevents price from being driven all the way to MC and instead leads to perpetual price fluctuation ("Edgeworth cycle") rather than a stable equilibrium.

10 — OLIGOPOLY

Oligopoly Models

Oligopoly
A market with a few large sellers, each aware that its decisions materially affect, and are affected by, rivals' decisions — the defining feature is mutual interdependence.

Kinked Demand Curve Model (Sweezy, 1939)

Core idea
Explains price rigidity in oligopoly. Each firm believes: (i) if it raises price, rivals will not follow (so it loses substantial market share — demand is elastic above the current price); (ii) if it cuts price, rivals will match the cut (so demand is inelastic below the current price).
Limitation
The model explains price rigidity once a price is established, but does not explain how the initial (kink) price is determined in the first place.

Collusive Models — Cartels

Cartel
A formal agreement among oligopolists to act jointly (fix price and/or output) as if they were a single monopolist, maximising joint industry profit.

Cartel equilibrium: set industry MR = industry MC (as a monopolist would), then allocate output among members (often by individual MC, so each member's MC = industry MC). Cartels are inherently unstable — each member has an incentive to secretly cheat (produce more than its quota) since its own MC is below the agreed price.

Price Leadership Model

Dominant Firm Price Leadership
A large, low-cost dominant firm sets the market price; smaller firms ("price followers" / competitive fringe) simply accept this price and sell as much as they wish at it, since they are too small to influence price individually.

The dominant firm's demand = market demand minus the fringe firms' combined supply at each price; it sets MR (of this residual demand) = its own MC.

Game Theory Basics in Oligopoly

Nash Equilibrium
A set of strategies (one per player) such that no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing strategy, given what the others are doing.
Table 10.1 — Classic Prisoner's Dilemma payoff matrix (Firm profits, in ₹ crore)
Firm A \ Firm BB: Low Price (Cheat)B: High Price (Collude)
A: Low Price (Cheat)A=5, B=5A=10, B=1
A: High Price (Collude)A=1, B=10A=8, B=8

The dominant strategy for both firms is to cheat (low price), yielding the Nash Equilibrium (5,5) — even though mutual collusion (8,8) would make both better off. This captures why cartels are hard to sustain without enforcement.

Exam angle
Distinguish clearly: Cournot = simultaneous quantity game (Nash equilibrium in quantities); Bertrand = simultaneous price game; Stackelberg = sequential quantity game (first-mover advantage); Kinked demand = explains rigidity, not initial price determination; Cartel = explicit collusion, monopoly-like joint outcome.

12 — EXCHANGE EFFICIENCY

Efficiency in Exchange: The Edgeworth Box

Edgeworth Box (Pure Exchange)
A diagram representing the allocation of two fixed goods (X, Y) between two consumers (A, B) — the box's dimensions are the total endowments of each good; each consumer's indifference curves are drawn from opposite corners.
Condition for efficiency in exchange
MRSxyA = MRSxyB    (both consumers' marginal rates of substitution between the two goods are equal)
Contract Curve
The locus of all Pareto-efficient allocations in the Edgeworth Box — every point off this curve permits a mutually beneficial trade that makes at least one party better off without harming the other; every point on it does not.
Pareto Efficiency (Optimality)
An allocation is Pareto efficient if no reallocation can make anyone better off without making someone else worse off.
Exam angle
If MRSA ≠ MRSB at some allocation, the two consumers can always find a trade that benefits both (moving toward the contract curve) — this is the intuitive proof of why unequal MRS implies inefficiency.
13 — PRODUCTION EFFICIENCY

Efficiency in Production

The identical Edgeworth Box logic applies to production, replacing consumers with two firms/industries and goods with two inputs (L, K), and indifference curves with isoquants.

Condition for efficiency in production
MRTSLKX = MRTSLKY    (both industries' marginal rates of technical substitution between the two inputs are equal)

The corresponding contract curve traces out all input allocations between industry X and industry Y that are technically efficient — this set of efficient output combinations (X, Y), when plotted in output space, generates the Production Possibility Frontier (PPF).

14 — TOP-LEVEL EFFICIENCY

Overall (Top-Level) Efficiency: MRS = MRT

Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT)
MRTxy = the (absolute) slope of the Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) — how much of good Y must be sacrificed to produce one more unit of good X, given full and efficient resource use. MRTxy = MCx/MCy.
Condition for overall (top-level) efficiency
MRSxy = MRTxy   (for every consumer)

This is the "grand" efficiency condition combining exchange and production efficiency: the rate at which consumers are willing to trade X for Y (MRS) must equal the rate at which the economy is able to transform X into Y (MRT) via production.

Table 14.1 — Three conditions for Pareto (economy-wide) efficiency
#ConditionEnsures
1Efficiency in Exchange: MRSA=MRSBGoods are optimally distributed among consumers
2Efficiency in Production: MRTSX=MRTSYInputs are optimally allocated among industries
3Overall/Top-level Efficiency: MRS=MRTThe right mix of goods is produced, matching consumer preferences
15 — WELFARE ECONOMICS

Welfare Economics & the Fundamental Theorems

The Two Fundamental Theorems of Welfare Economics

First Theorem
Every competitive equilibrium (under perfect competition, with no externalities/public goods) is Pareto efficient. ("The invisible hand" formalised.)
Second Theorem
Any Pareto-efficient allocation can be achieved as a competitive equilibrium, provided an appropriate initial redistribution of endowments (lump-sum transfers) is carried out first.
Exam angle
First theorem = efficiency claim (markets are efficient). Second theorem = equity-efficiency separation claim (any distributionally-desired efficient outcome is achievable via lump-sum redistribution + free markets, without needing direct price controls). This distinction is frequently tested.

Utility Possibility Frontier (UPF) & Social Welfare

Utility Possibility Frontier
Plots the maximum utility one individual (B) can achieve for every given level of utility of another individual (A), given a fixed allocation of resources — derived by tracing all points on the contract curve into utility space.
Grand Utility Possibility Frontier (GUPF)
The outer envelope of UPFs corresponding to every possible efficient production point (i.e., incorporating both exchange and production efficiency) — the ultimate utility frontier for the economy.
Social Welfare Function (SWF)
A function (Bergson-Samuelson) that ranks different distributions of utility across individuals according to society's value judgements — W = W(UA, UB). The point where the highest social indifference curve (from the SWF) is tangent to the GUPF is the social welfare optimum.

Compensation Principles (comparing welfare when there are both gainers and losers)

16 — MARKET FAILURE

Externality & Market Failure

Externality
A cost or benefit affecting a third party who did not choose to incur it, arising from the production or consumption of a good — i.e., a spillover effect not reflected in market prices.
Table 16.1 — Four types of externality
TypeEffect on third partyExample
Negative Production ExternalityHarm from a firm's production activityFactory pollution affecting nearby residents
Positive Production ExternalityBenefit from a firm's production activityA beekeeper's bees pollinating a neighbouring orchard
Negative Consumption ExternalityHarm from someone's consumptionSecond-hand smoke, loud music
Positive Consumption ExternalityBenefit from someone's consumptionGetting vaccinated (herd immunity), education (informed citizenry)
Core distinction
Marginal Private Cost/Benefit (MPC / MPB) — borne/enjoyed by the decision-maker only
Marginal Social Cost/Benefit (MSC / MSB) — includes the externality
With a negative production externality: MSC = MPC + Marginal External Cost
With a positive consumption externality: MSB = MPB + Marginal External Benefit

Correcting Externalities

Table 16.2 — Policy remedies
RemedyHow it worksBest suited for
Pigouvian TaxA per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost, imposed on producers/consumers to force them to internalise the externality (shifts MPC up to MSC)Negative externalities (e.g., carbon/pollution tax)
Pigouvian SubsidyA per-unit subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit, to raise private incentive to the socially optimal levelPositive externalities (e.g., education/vaccination subsidies)
Coase TheoremIf property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs are zero, private bargaining between the affected parties can achieve the efficient outcome regardless of who initially holds the property rightSmall-numbers bargaining situations (e.g., a factory and a single neighbouring farm)
Regulation / QuotasDirect government limits on quantity produced/pollution emittedCases where price-based tools are hard to calibrate or enforce
Tradable PermitsGovernment sets a total quantity (cap) of allowable externality (e.g., emissions) and allows firms to trade permitsCombines quantity certainty with market-based efficiency ("cap-and-trade")
Coase Theorem — statement
When transaction costs are negligible and property rights are well-defined, the parties affected by an externality can bargain to an efficient outcome privately, without government intervention — and the final efficient allocation of resources is independent of which party is initially assigned the property right (though the distribution of wealth between them differs).
Real-world limitation of Coase
Breaks down when transaction costs are high, property rights are ambiguous, or there are many affected parties (e.g., climate change, air pollution affecting millions) — making Pigouvian taxes/regulation more practical at scale.

Public Goods — a related market failure (brief)

Public Good
A good that is both non-excludable (cannot prevent non-payers from consuming it) and non-rivalrous (one person's consumption doesn't reduce availability for others) — e.g., national defence, street lighting. Leads to the free-rider problem and under-provision by private markets.
Exam angle
Negative externality ⇒ market over-produces relative to the social optimum; positive externality ⇒ market under-produces relative to the social optimum. Always link the diagram direction (MSC above MPC, or MSB above MPB) to whether output should be taxed down or subsidised up.

19 — SELF-TEST

Practice MCQs

Click each question to reveal the answer & explanation. Extended bank covering both Parts A and B.

Q1. Under perfect competition, a firm's short-run supply curve is: Recall
(a) The entire MC curve   (b) The MC curve above (and including) minimum AVC   (c) The AC curve above its minimum   (d) A horizontal line at market price

Answer: (b) — below minimum AVC the firm shuts down rather than supplying anything.
Q2. A profit-maximising monopolist will never produce where price elasticity of demand is: Conceptual
(a) Greater than 1   (b) Equal to 1   (c) Less than 1   (d) Infinite

Answer: (c) — MR would be negative there (since MR=P(1−1/e)), while MC is always positive, so MR=MC cannot hold.
Q3. In third-degree price discrimination, the market with the more elastic demand is charged: Application
(a) A higher price   (b) A lower price   (c) The same price as other markets   (d) Zero price

Answer: (b) — price and elasticity are inversely related across segments under third-degree discrimination.
Q4. The "excess capacity theorem" is associated with which market structure? Recall
(a) Perfect competition   (b) Monopoly   (c) Monopolistic competition   (d) Oligopoly

Answer: (c) — the long-run tangency of AR and AC occurs to the left of AC's minimum point, leaving the firm with unused (excess) capacity.
Q5. In the Cournot duopoly model, each firm assumes that: Conceptual
(a) The rival will match any price cut   (b) The rival's output quantity will remain unchanged   (c) The rival is a price leader   (d) There is perfect collusion

Answer: (b) — this is the defining "naive conjecture" of the Cournot model.
Q6. The Bertrand Paradox refers to the result that: Application
(a) Duopoly price settles above monopoly price   (b) With homogeneous products and price competition, just two firms can drive price down to marginal cost   (c) Bertrand competition always leads to collusion   (d) Price never changes under Bertrand competition

Answer: (b) — the paradox is that competitive-market outcomes (P=MC) emerge even with only two firms.
Q7. In the Stackelberg model, the "leader" firm's profit compared to the Cournot outcome is: Comparative
(a) Lower   (b) Higher   (c) Exactly equal   (d) Zero

Answer: (b) — moving first and committing to an output gives the leader a strategic ("first-mover") advantage over the simultaneous Cournot game.
Q8. The kinked demand curve model primarily explains: Conceptual
(a) How the initial oligopoly price is determined   (b) Why prices tend to be sticky/rigid once set in an oligopoly   (c) Why oligopolists always collude   (d) Why monopoly output is inefficient

Answer: (b) — it is silent on how the original kink price is arrived at; it only explains rigidity around that price.
Q9. The efficiency condition in a pure exchange economy (Edgeworth Box) is: Recall
(a) MRTSX = MRTSY   (b) MRSA = MRSB   (c) MRS = MRT   (d) MC = AC

Answer: (b) — both consumers' marginal rates of substitution between the two goods must be equal at every point on the contract curve.
Q10. The "Second Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics" states that: Advanced
(a) All competitive equilibria are Pareto efficient   (b) Any Pareto-efficient allocation can be reached as a competitive equilibrium, given an appropriate lump-sum redistribution of initial endowments   (c) Monopoly is always inefficient   (d) Externalities always lead to market failure

Answer: (b) — (a) describes the First Theorem instead.
Q11. A negative production externality (e.g., factory pollution) typically leads the free market to: Diagram-based
(a) Under-produce relative to the social optimum   (b) Over-produce relative to the social optimum   (c) Produce exactly the efficient quantity   (d) Shut down entirely

Answer: (b) — since MPC < MSC, the market equilibrium (where D=MPC) occurs at a higher quantity than the social optimum (where D=MSC).
Q12. The Coase Theorem requires which key condition to achieve an efficient outcome through private bargaining? Conceptual
(a) Government price controls   (b) Well-defined property rights and negligible transaction costs   (c) A large number of affected parties   (d) A Pigouvian tax

Answer: (b) — with these conditions, the identity of the property-right holder affects the distribution of gains but not the efficiency of the final outcome.
Q13. The correct remedy for a positive externality (e.g., education) is generally: Application
(a) A Pigouvian tax   (b) A Pigouvian subsidy   (c) A production quota   (d) No intervention needed

Answer: (b) — a subsidy raises private incentive toward the socially optimal (higher) level of the activity.
Q14. Which of the following is/are true about a "public good"? Assertion-set
I. It is non-rivalrous in consumption
II. It is non-excludable
III. Private markets tend to under-provide it due to free-riding
IV. It always generates a negative externality

(a) I & II only   (b) I, II & III only   (c) All four   (d) III & IV only

Q15. Overall (top-level) efficiency in a two-good economy requires: Recall
(a) MRTSX=MRTSY only   (b) MRSA=MRSB only   (c) MRSxy = MRTxy   (d) AR=MR for all firms

Answer: (c) — this is the third and final condition, linking consumer preferences (MRS) with the economy's production trade-off (MRT).
Q16. In a cartel, individual members have an incentive to secretly produce more than their agreed quota because: Application
(a) Their MC exceeds the cartel price   (b) Their individual MC is below the agreed (monopoly-like) cartel price, making extra output profitable for them alone   (c) Cartels are illegal   (d) Demand is perfectly inelastic

Answer: (b) — this incentive to "cheat" is exactly what makes cartels inherently unstable without strong enforcement mechanisms.
Q17. In the dominant-firm price leadership model, smaller "fringe" firms: Conceptual
(a) Set the market price independently   (b) Accept the price set by the dominant firm and sell as much as they wish at it   (c) Always merge with the dominant firm   (d) Set a lower price than the dominant firm

Answer: (b) — they behave as price-takers with respect to the dominant firm's chosen price.
Q18. Which welfare criterion requires the compensation test to hold in both directions to avoid contradictory rankings? Advanced
(a) Pareto criterion   (b) Kaldor criterion   (c) Hicks criterion   (d) Scitovsky criterion

Answer: (d) — the Scitovsky criterion was devised precisely to resolve the "Scitovsky Paradox" that can arise from applying Kaldor-Hicks in only one direction.
