INDIAN ECONOMY · POLICY DEEP-DIVE

The New Economic Policy, 1991

A full, ground-up treatment of India's defining economic turning point: the crisis that forced it, the Liberalization-Privatization-Globalization package that answered it, sector-by-sector reform detail (industry, trade, finance, fiscal, public sector, FDI), the first-vs-second-generation reform sequence, and a rounded assessment of what changed — and what didn't.

Part A

Why 1991? Background & the Crisis

01 — ROOT CAUSES

Background: Structural Weaknesses of the Planning Model

The 1991 crisis was not a sudden accident — it was the culmination of structural weaknesses that had been building for over a decade within the planning-era mixed economy.

Table 1.1 — Accumulated structural weaknesses (1980s)
WeaknessNature
Chronic, widening fiscal deficitsGovernment expenditure (subsidies, public sector losses, administration) persistently outpaced revenue mobilisation through the 1980s
Growing external commercial borrowingIndia increasingly financed its current account deficit via costly, short-term external commercial borrowings rather than stable long-term flows (FDI was minimal)
Inefficient, loss-making public sectorMany public sector enterprises operated at chronic losses, becoming a fiscal drain rather than a source of investible surplus
"Licence-Permit-Quota Raj"Extensive industrial licensing suppressed competition, discouraged scale efficiency, and encouraged rent-seeking rather than productivity growth
Import-substitution exhaustionDecades of inward-looking industrialization left Indian industry globally uncompetitive, with limited export dynamism to earn foreign exchange
Overvalued, inflexible exchange rateMade Indian exports less competitive while keeping imports relatively cheap, worsening the trade balance
Exam angle
A strong answer frames 1991 as the point where long-building structural weaknesses met an external shock (Gulf War) — not as a reform that could have been avoided by better luck alone; the underlying model itself had become unsustainable.
02 — THE CRISIS

The 1991 Crisis in Detail

Timeline of the acute crisis

Table 2.1 — Key events, 1990-91
EventEffect
Gulf War (Aug 1990 – Feb 1991)Oil price spike raised India's import bill sharply; disrupted remittances and evacuation costs from Indian workers in the Gulf
Political instability (frequent government changes, 1989-91)Undermined investor and creditor confidence in India's policy continuity
Credit rating downgrades by international agenciesMade fresh external commercial borrowing far more difficult and expensive
Non-Resident Indian (NRI) deposit withdrawalsAccelerated the drain on foreign exchange reserves as confidence collapsed
Reserves fall to ~$1.2 billion (barely 2-3 weeks of import cover) by June 1991India stood on the brink of sovereign default on external payment obligations
Gold pledged as collateral (Bank of England, Union Bank of Switzerland), 1991Emergency measure to raise foreign exchange and signal creditworthiness, averting imminent default
IMF Structural Adjustment loan negotiatedCame with conditionalities pushing toward the liberalization package ultimately announced
A sovereign default was narrowly avoided
The severity of mid-1991 is hard to overstate: without the gold pledge and emergency IMF assistance, India would very likely have defaulted on its external payment obligations — a scenario with severe, long-lasting consequences for India's international creditworthiness and economic sovereignty.
03 — THE SHIFT

Philosophy: From Plan to Market

Table 3.1 — The underlying philosophical shift
DimensionPre-1991 (Planning-era) philosophyPost-1991 (NEP) philosophy
Role of the stateDirect producer/owner in "commanding heights" sectorsRegulator and facilitator; direct production role reduced
Resource allocationAdministrative allocation (licensing, quotas)Market-based allocation, price signals
Trade orientationInward-looking, import substitutionOutward-looking, export promotion & global integration
Foreign capitalViewed with suspicion; tightly restrictedActively courted (FDI/FII) as a growth driver
CompetitionSuppressed (licensing, reservation)Encouraged as an efficiency driver
Exam angle
This is not merely a technical policy shift — it reflects a genuine change in development philosophy, from state-led import-substitution industrialization to market-led, globally-integrated growth — a shift that, while triggered by crisis, has broadly persisted (with adjustments) across subsequent governments of differing political orientations, suggesting a degree of durable political-economy consensus.
Part B

The Reform Package, Sector by Sector

04 — INDUSTRY

Industrial Policy, 1991

Table 4.1 — Specific measures
MeasureDetail
Abolition of industrial licensingRemoved for all industries except a short negative list (initially ~18 industries retained for security/strategic/environmental/health reasons — later pruned further)
Reduction of public-sector-reserved industriesFrom 17 industries reserved exclusively for the public sector, down to 8 initially, then progressively fewer over subsequent years — by the 2000s, only atomic energy and railway operations remained fully reserved
Automatic approval for foreign technology/equityAutomatic approval up to specified thresholds (e.g., 51% foreign equity in many industries) replaced case-by-case government clearance for a wide range of sectors
MRTP Act amendmentsRemoved the requirement for large ("MRTP") firms to seek prior government approval before expanding capacity or undertaking mergers — the asset-size threshold trigger was abolished
Location policy liberalizationRelaxed restrictions requiring government approval for setting up industrial units in/near large cities
05 — TRADE

Trade Policy Reforms

  • Sharp cuts in customs tariffs — peak import tariff rates, which had exceeded 200-300% in the 1980s on some items, were brought down in successive budgets to double digits by the 2000s
  • Removal of import licensing for most capital goods and intermediate goods; quantitative restrictions on most consumer goods were phased out more gradually, largely eliminated by 2001 (partly under WTO commitments)
  • Export incentive schemes rationalised and expanded (Duty Drawback, Export Promotion Capital Goods scheme, Export Processing/Special Economic Zones)
  • India became a founding member of the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 1995, binding many of these trade liberalization commitments internationally
Exam angle
Note the gradual, phased nature of trade liberalization — quantitative restrictions on many consumer goods persisted through the 1990s and were fully removed only around 2001, distinct from the much faster, immediate abolition of industrial licensing in 1991 itself.
06 — EXCHANGE RATE

Exchange Rate Reforms

Table 6.1 — Steps toward a market-determined rupee
StepYearDetail
Two-step rupee devaluationJuly 1991Cumulative devaluation of roughly 18-19% against major currencies, correcting prior overvaluation
LERMS (Liberalized Exchange Rate Management System)1992A dual exchange rate mechanism — partial market-determined rate for a portion of foreign exchange transactions, transitional step toward full convertibility
Unified market-determined exchange rate1993LERMS's dual rate replaced by a single, market-determined exchange rate
Full Current Account Convertibility1994Rupee made fully convertible for current account transactions (trade, remittances), accepting IMF Article VIII obligations
Capital Account ConvertibilityOngoing/partialIndia has moved toward greater, but still not full, capital account convertibility — remaining a cautious, calibrated process given lessons from other countries' currency crises (e.g., the 1997 East Asian crisis)
07 — BANKING

Financial Sector (Banking) Reforms

Narasimham Committee I (1991) & II (1998)
Two landmark committee reports (chaired by M. Narasimham, a former RBI Governor) that shaped India's banking-sector reform agenda.
Table 7.1 — Key recommendations implemented
Reform areaChange
Statutory pre-emptionsGradual reduction of the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) from very high levels, freeing up more bank funds for commercial lending rather than mandatory government securities holding
Interest rate deregulationProgressive deregulation of lending and deposit interest rates, previously administratively controlled
Prudential normsIntroduction of international (Basel-aligned) capital adequacy norms, income recognition and asset classification (IRAC) norms for non-performing assets (NPAs)
New private sector banksLicences granted to a new generation of private banks from the mid-1990s (e.g., ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank, Axis Bank), ending the effective moratorium on new private banking since 1969 nationalization
Recapitalisation of public sector banksGovernment infusion of capital into weak public sector banks to meet new capital adequacy requirements
Debt Recovery TribunalsEstablished (from 1993) to speed up recovery of bad loans, later supplemented by the SARFAESI Act (2002) and eventually the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016)
08 — CAPITAL MARKETS

Capital Market Reforms

  • SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) — established administratively in 1988, given full statutory powers in 1992 (SEBI Act, 1992), directly in response to regulatory gaps exposed by the Harshad Mehta securities scam (1992)
  • Abolition of the Controller of Capital Issues (1992) — companies no longer needed government approval to determine share pricing for public issues, moving to a market-determined, disclosure-based regime
  • National Stock Exchange (NSE) established (1992, trading from 1994) — introduced screen-based electronic trading, dramatically improving transparency and efficiency versus the traditional open-outcry system
  • Dematerialisation of shares via the National Securities Depository Limited (NSDL, 1996) — eliminated risks associated with physical share certificates (theft, forgery, transfer delays)
  • Opening of capital markets to Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs) from 1992
09 — FISCAL POLICY

Fiscal Sector Reforms

  • Tax reform — reduction of peak personal income tax rates (from over 50% to more moderate levels) and corporate tax rates; simplification and widening of the tax base, based partly on recommendations of the Tax Reforms Committee (Raja Chelliah Committee, 1991)
  • Indirect tax rationalisation — gradual simplification of the excise and customs structure, a long process that eventually culminated in the unified Goods and Services Tax (GST) in July 2017 — the logical endpoint of decades of indirect tax reform begun in this era
  • Fiscal deficit reduction targets — efforts (with mixed and uneven success over the years) to reduce the fiscal deficit as a percentage of GDP, later institutionalised through the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003
  • Subsidy rationalisation — gradual, politically difficult attempts to reduce and better target food, fertiliser, and petroleum subsidies
Exam angle
GST (2017) is frequently framed as the culmination of the indirect tax reform process that began with the 1991 NEP — a useful long-arc connection for essay-type answers, even though it falls chronologically well outside the immediate 1991-93 reform window.
10 — PUBLIC SECTOR

Public Sector Reform & Disinvestment

Disinvestment
The sale of a portion (not necessarily majority/full) of government equity holding in Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) to private/public investors — distinct from full "privatization" (complete transfer of ownership/control), though India has, over time, also undertaken genuine strategic sales (full ownership transfer) in some cases.
  • Disinvestment began cautiously in 1991-92, initially through minority stake sales, with proceeds used partly to help fund the fiscal deficit
  • A separate Department of Disinvestment (later Department of Investment and Public Asset Management, DIPAM) was eventually created to manage the process systematically
  • Strategic sales (full/majority ownership transfer to private buyers) picked up pace in specific phases — e.g., several PSUs sold in the early 2000s (BALCO, Hindustan Zinc, VSNL)
  • Progress remained slower and more politically contentious than in industrial/trade liberalization — labour concerns, political resistance, and valuation disputes frequently delayed or diluted disinvestment plans
Exam angle
Distinguish carefully: disinvestment (partial equity sale, government retains control) is not the same as privatization (full transfer of ownership and control) — India's post-1991 approach has predominantly been the former, with genuine full privatization/strategic sale remaining comparatively rare and episodic.
11 — FOREIGN INVESTMENT

FDI & Foreign Investment Policy Evolution

Table 11.1 — Liberalization of the FDI regime over time
Period/EventChange
1991Automatic approval introduced for FDI up to 51% equity in a specified list of 34 "priority" industries
1997 onwardAutomatic approval route progressively extended to more sectors and higher equity caps
1999Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) replaced the older, more restrictive Foreign Exchange Regulation Act (FERA, 1973) — a shift in tone from "regulation/control" to "management" of foreign exchange
1999-2000Insurance sector opened to private and foreign participation (up to specified caps) following the setting up of the IRDA (Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority), ending the decades-long LIC/GIC state monopoly
2000s onwardProgressive relaxation of FDI caps across telecom, civil aviation, retail (multi-brand retail FDI permitted with conditions from 2012), defence, and other sectors, in phases, across successive governments
Exam angle
FERA → FEMA (1999) is a frequently tested transition — FERA (1973) treated foreign exchange dealings as something to be tightly regulated/restricted (with criminal penalties for violations), while FEMA (1999) reframed it as something to be managed/facilitated for a liberalizing economy (civil penalties, not criminal, for most violations) — a symbolic and substantive shift in regulatory philosophy.
Part C

Sequencing, Impact & Assessment

12 — SEQUENCING

First-Generation vs Second-Generation Reforms

Table 12.1 — Two broad phases of reform
First-Generation Reforms (early-mid 1990s)Second-Generation Reforms (late 1990s onward)
FocusMacro-stabilisation and initial liberalization — de-licensing, devaluation, initial trade opening, initial financial deregulationDeeper, more structural/institutional reforms — factor markets (land, labour), public sector restructuring, state-level reforms, infrastructure regulation
NatureRelatively easier to implement quickly (administrative/executive decisions, fewer entrenched interest-group veto points)Politically harder — require legislative change, touch entrenched interests (labour laws, land acquisition, agricultural marketing), often state-government-dependent
ExamplesIndustrial de-licensing, rupee devaluation, initial tariff cuts, SEBI, initial bank reformsLabour law reform (slow, politically difficult, partly addressed via the 2020 Labour Codes), agricultural market reforms (attempted, contentious), disinvestment/privatization, GST (2017), Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016)
Exam angle
A very common essay framing: India completed first-generation reforms relatively successfully and quickly, but second-generation reforms have been slower, more contested, and remain incomplete in several areas (labour markets, land acquisition, agricultural marketing) even decades later — this "incomplete reform" framing is central to most balanced assessments of the NEP's legacy.
13 — GROWTH IMPACT

Impact: Growth & Structural Change

Structural shifts accelerated by reform

  • Accelerated growth of the services sector, especially IT/ITeS, benefiting from global integration and reduced regulatory friction
  • Significant expansion of foreign exchange reserves — from crisis-level near-zero in 1991 to a far more comfortable cushion in subsequent decades, providing resilience against later external shocks
  • Diversification of the export basket and trading partners, reducing dependence on any single market
  • Emergence of globally competitive Indian firms in select sectors (IT services, pharmaceuticals, auto components)

IndicatorGeneral findingKey debate

15 — CRITICISMS

Criticisms of the NEP

  • Agriculture neglected — the reform agenda focused heavily on industry, trade, and finance for many years, with agricultural market reforms (APMC Act changes, etc.) coming much later and remaining highly contested (as seen in the 2020-21 farm laws controversy)
  • Incomplete factor market reform — labour and land markets remained heavily regulated for decades after 1991, creating a mismatch between liberalized product/capital markets and rigid factor markets
  • Jobless growth concerns — critics argue the growth model has not generated employment at a pace matching the growing working-age population (linking to the demographic dividend concerns in the Population notes)
  • Crony capitalism concerns — critics argue that liberalization sometimes replaced state-controlled rent-seeking (licence raj) with new forms of concentrated corporate influence over policy (natural resource allocation controversies of the 2010s, for instance)
  • External vulnerability — greater integration into global capital markets exposes India to capital flow reversals and global financial contagion (evident during episodes like the 2013 "Taper Tantrum")
  • Uneven regional/social benefit — as noted in Section 14, gains have not been evenly distributed
The IMF/World Bank "conditionality" critique
Some critics frame the 1991 reforms as externally imposed via IMF/World Bank loan conditionalities rather than a genuinely home-grown policy choice — defenders counter that the underlying reform logic (de-licensing, trade opening, fiscal discipline) had already been building as a domestic policy consensus through the 1980s, and the crisis merely accelerated/forced through changes that were arguably overdue on their own economic merits.
16 — COMPARATIVE VIEW

NEP in Comparative Perspective

Table 16.1 — India's reform path vs other major developing/transition economies
Country/RegionReform characterContrast with India
China (from 1978, Deng Xiaoping reforms)Gradual, sequenced, export-manufacturing-led, retained significant state control over key levers (currency, land ownership)China began reforms over a decade earlier, under less acute crisis pressure, with a stronger initial manufacturing/export push; India's reform was crisis-triggered and services-led
Former Soviet Bloc ("shock therapy" transitions, e.g., Russia, early 1990s)Very rapid, simultaneous liberalization of prices, trade, and privatizationIndia's approach was comparatively more gradual/calibrated (especially in capital account opening and privatization), often credited with avoiding some of the severe transitional output collapses seen in shock-therapy economies
East Asian "Tiger" economies (earlier decades)Strong export-oriented manufacturing push, often with significant state industrial policy guidanceIndia's post-1991 growth has been comparatively more services-led, with manufacturing's share of GDP/employment rising much more modestly than in the East Asian experience
Exam angle
India's reform path is often characterised as "gradualist" — a useful contrast term against the more abrupt "shock therapy" approach of some transition economies, and worth having ready for comparative-essay-style questions.

Q1. Which committee's recommendations most directly shaped India's post-1991 banking sector reforms? Recall
(a) Chelliah Committee   (b) Narasimham Committee   (c) Fowler Committee   (d) Hilton Young Commission

Answer: (b) — Narasimham Committee I (1991) and II (1998).
Q2. FEMA replaced FERA in which year, reflecting a shift from "regulation" to "management" of foreign exchange? Recall
(a) 1991   (b) 1995   (c) 1999   (d) 2003

Answer: (c).
Q3. "Disinvestment" in the Indian public sector context refers to: Conceptual
(a) Complete closure of a PSU   (b) Sale of a portion of government equity holding in a PSU, with government typically retaining some control   (c) Nationalization of a private firm   (d) A ban on foreign investment

Answer: (b) — distinct from full privatization/strategic sale.
Q4. India achieved full current account convertibility of the rupee in: Recall
(a) 1991   (b) 1992   (c) 1994   (d) 2000

Answer: (c) — after the transitional LERMS (1992) and unified market rate (1993).
Q5. Which of the following best describes India's approach to economic reform, compared to the "shock therapy" of some transition economies? Application
(a) Equally rapid and simultaneous across all fronts   (b) Comparatively gradual and calibrated, especially regarding capital account opening and privatization   (c) Entirely state-controlled with no market elements   (d) Focused solely on agriculture

Answer: (b).
Q6. "Second-generation reforms" in the Indian context primarily refer to: Conceptual
(a) The initial 1991 de-licensing measures   (b) Deeper structural reforms in factor markets (land, labour), the public sector, and state-level institutions, generally slower and more contested   (c) The Green Revolution   (d) Bank nationalization

Answer: (b).
Q7. The Goods and Services Tax (GST), implemented in 2017, is best understood as: Application
(a) An entirely new, unrelated policy with no link to 1991   (b) The culmination of the indirect tax reform and rationalisation process that began with the 1991 NEP   (c) A reversal of the 1991 reforms   (d) A purely agricultural policy

Answer: (b).
Q8. Which sector, previously a state monopoly (LIC/GIC), was opened to private and foreign participation around 1999-2000? Recall
(a) Banking   (b) Insurance   (c) Railways   (d) Coal mining

Answer: (b) — following the establishment of the IRDA.
Q9. A commonly cited criticism of post-1991 reforms is that: Application
(a) Agriculture and factor market (land/labour) reforms lagged well behind industrial and trade liberalization   (b) Industrial licensing was never actually abolished   (c) The fiscal deficit was eliminated immediately   (d) Foreign investment was completely banned after 1991

Answer: (a).
Q10. The National Stock Exchange (NSE), established in 1992, is significant mainly because it: Conceptual
(a) Was India's first-ever stock exchange   (b) Introduced modern, transparent, screen-based electronic trading, improving efficiency over the older open-outcry system   (c) Banned all foreign investors   (d) Replaced the Reserve Bank of India

Answer: (b).
Q11. Which of the following is true regarding India's foreign exchange reserves before and after the 1991 reforms? Comparative
(a) They fell continuously after 1991   (b) They rose substantially over subsequent decades from the crisis-level lows of 1991, providing greater resilience to later shocks   (c) They remained unchanged   (d) India stopped holding foreign exchange reserves after 1991

Answer: (b).
Q12. The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, which institutionalised fiscal deficit targets, was enacted in: Recall
(a) 1991   (b) 1998   (c) 2003   (d) 2010

Answer: (c).
