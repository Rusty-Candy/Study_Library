Part A

Public Goods & Market Failure

01 — CLASSIFICATION

Public vs Private Goods: The Classification

Goods are classified along two independent dimensions — rivalry (does one person's consumption reduce what's available to others?) and excludability (can non-payers be prevented from consuming it?) — giving a four-way matrix.

Table 1.1 — The four-way goods classification matrix
ExcludableNon-Excludable
RivalrousPrivate Goods — food, clothing, a haircutCommon Pool Resources — ocean fish stocks, groundwater, open grazing land
Non-RivalrousClub Goods — cable TV, a toll road (uncongested), a private park with membershipPure Public Goods — national defence, lighthouse, street lighting, clean air (locally)
Rivalry (Rivalrousness)
One person's consumption of a unit of the good reduces the quantity available for others (e.g., eating an apple).
Excludability
It is possible (at reasonable cost) to prevent someone who hasn't paid from consuming the good (e.g., a locked cinema requiring a ticket).
Pure Public Good
A good that is both non-rivalrous (one person's consumption doesn't diminish another's) and non-excludable (impossible/very costly to prevent anyone from consuming it once provided) — e.g., national defence, a lighthouse's beam, street lighting, basic scientific research.

Two key properties of pure public goods

  • Non-rivalry implies the marginal cost of serving one more person is zero — efficiency requires a zero (or near-zero) price, which conflicts with the need to fund provision through some payment mechanism
  • Non-excludability means the market cannot exclude non-payers, so private firms have no way to charge for it profitably — hence the market under-provides or fails to provide it at all
Exam angle
A common trap: not everything the government provides is a "public good" in the technical sense (e.g., a government-run hospital bed is rivalrous and excludable — a private-good-like service merely provided by the state for equity reasons). Conversely, some privately-run goods are technically public goods (a privately-funded fireworks display, freely visible to all, is non-rivalrous and non-excludable). The classification is about the good's inherent economic characteristics, not who provides it.
02 — THE FREE RIDER PROBLEM

The Free Rider Problem & Provision of Public Goods

Free Rider Problem
Because non-excludability prevents charging non-payers, each individual has an incentive to understate their true willingness to pay (or not pay at all) for a public good, hoping to enjoy it anyway once others fund its provision — leading to systematic under-provision relative to the socially efficient quantity, if left entirely to private/voluntary contribution.
Efficient provision condition for a public good (Samuelson condition)
Σ MRSi = MRT   — the sum of all individuals' marginal rates of substitution (willingness to pay) for the public good must equal the marginal rate of transformation (marginal cost of provision)

Contrast with private goods, where efficiency requires each individual's MRS = MRT (price) separately — for public goods, since everyone consumes the same unit simultaneously (non-rivalry), their willingnesses-to-pay are added vertically, not chosen individually at a common price.

Why government provision is the standard solution

  • The state can fund provision through compulsory taxation, bypassing the free-rider problem inherent in voluntary payment
  • Alternative/partial solutions: club arrangements (converting non-excludable goods into excludable "club goods" via technology, e.g., subscription/paywall models), social norms and community-based provision (in small groups, per Elinor Ostrom's work on common-pool resource management), and Coasean private bargaining in small-numbers cases
Exam angle
The Samuelson condition (Σ MRS = MRT) is one of the most important formula-based distinctions in this topic — remember it contrasts directly with the private-good efficiency condition from General Equilibrium (MRS = MRT for each individual), covered in the Market Structures & Welfare notes.
03 — EXTERNALITIES

Externalities: Quick Recap in the Public Economics Frame

Externalities were covered in depth in the "Market Structures & General Equilibrium, Efficiency and Welfare" notes (Section 16) — this section recaps the core points as they apply specifically to public economics and government intervention.

Externality
A cost or benefit affecting a third party, not reflected in market prices — negative (pollution) or positive (education, vaccination).
Table 3.1 — Government correction tools
ToolUsed forMechanism
Pigouvian TaxNegative externalityPer-unit tax = marginal external cost, shifting MPC to MSC
Pigouvian SubsidyPositive externalityPer-unit subsidy = marginal external benefit, shifting MPB to MSB
Coase Theorem (private bargaining)Either type, with well-defined property rights & low transaction costsAffected parties bargain directly to the efficient outcome
Regulation/Quotas, Tradable PermitsNegative externality, especially large-scale (e.g., emissions)Direct quantity limits, or a capped, tradable permit market
Exam angle
Externalities and public goods are both classic forms of market failure justifying government intervention — but they are conceptually distinct: an externality is a spillover from an otherwise normal (rival, excludable) transaction; a public good's non-provision stems from its own inherent non-rivalry/non-excludability, with no "normal" private transaction to spill over from in the first place.
Part B

The Government Budget

04 — BUDGET STRUCTURE

Structure of the Government Budget (Indian Framework)

Government Budget
An annual financial statement of the government's estimated receipts and expenditure for the coming fiscal year (in India, April-March), presented to Parliament under Article 112 of the Constitution (termed the "Annual Financial Statement").
Table 4.1 — The two-part budget structure
Revenue BudgetCapital Budget
ReceiptsRevenue Receipts: Tax revenue (income tax, GST, customs, excise) + Non-tax revenue (interest receipts, dividends/profits from PSUs, fees) — do not create a liability or reduce an assetCapital Receipts: Market borrowings, recovery of loans, disinvestment proceeds — do either create a liability (borrowing) or reduce an asset (disinvestment, loan recovery)
ExpenditureRevenue Expenditure: Day-to-day/consumption spending — salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies — does not create an assetCapital Expenditure: Spending that creates an asset or reduces a liability — infrastructure, machinery, loans given out, defence equipment
Exam angle
The test for classifying any budget item: does it create/reduce an asset or liability? If yes → Capital item. If no (routine, consumption-like) → Revenue item. Interest payments are revenue expenditure (routine, no asset created), but a loan given out by the government is capital expenditure (creates a financial asset — the right to future repayment).
05 — BUDGET BALANCE

Types of Budget Balance

Table 5.1 — Balanced, surplus, and deficit budgets
TypeConditionImplication
Balanced BudgetTotal Receipts = Total ExpenditureNo net borrowing requirement; classical/conservative fiscal ideal, rarely achieved in modern practice
Surplus BudgetTotal Receipts > Total ExpenditureGovernment saves; can be used to pay down existing debt; rare, sometimes used to cool an overheating economy
Deficit BudgetTotal Expenditure > Total ReceiptsGovernment must borrow to finance the gap; the norm for most modern economies, especially developing ones with large investment needs
Part C

Deficits & Public Debt

06 — DEFICIT MEASURES

Measures of Deficit — The Full Family

Table 6.1 — The deficit hierarchy (India-specific terminology)
Deficit measureFormulaWhat it captures
Revenue DeficitRevenue Expenditure − Revenue ReceiptsGovernment is unable to cover even its routine/consumption spending from current income — has to borrow just to meet day-to-day expenses (widely seen as fiscally unhealthy)
Fiscal DeficitTotal Expenditure − Total Receipts (excluding borrowings)The government's total borrowing requirement for the year — the single most-watched deficit indicator
Primary DeficitFiscal Deficit − Interest PaymentsThe borrowing requirement excluding the burden of past debt — isolates the deficit generated by the current year's fiscal decisions alone
Effective Revenue DeficitRevenue Deficit − Grants for Creation of Capital AssetsA refinement recognising that some "revenue expenditure" (grants to states for capital projects) still creates real assets, even though classified as revenue spending
Monetized DeficitPortion of the deficit financed by borrowing directly from the RBI (money creation)Historically important (pre-1997 "ad hoc Treasury Bills" system in India); largely discontinued as a formal mechanism after 1997
Exam angle
Primary Deficit = 0 means the government is borrowing exactly enough to cover interest on past debt — i.e., it has stabilised new net borrowing at zero for current operations, even though the Fiscal Deficit remains positive (equal to interest payments). This is a classic numerical/conceptual trap.
07 — FINANCING

Deficit Financing

Table 7.1 — How governments finance a fiscal deficit
MethodMechanismKey risk
Market borrowing (domestic)Issuing government bonds/securities (G-Secs) purchased by banks, institutions, individualsCan "crowd out" private investment by raising interest rates / absorbing loanable funds
External borrowingLoans from foreign governments, multilateral institutions (World Bank, IMF), or international bond issuanceExposes the country to exchange-rate risk and external payment obligations
Monetization (deficit financing via money creation)Government borrows directly from the central bank, effectively "printing money"Highly inflationary if used excessively; India moved away from automatic (ad hoc) monetization after 1997
Disinvestment / asset salesOne-off capital receipts from selling government equity/assetsNot a recurring/sustainable source; only a temporary bridge
Crowding Out
When government borrowing to finance its deficit raises interest rates (competing with private borrowers for loanable funds) or otherwise displaces private investment that would have occurred in the deficit's absence — a key argument against excessive reliance on domestic market borrowing.
08 — PUBLIC DEBT

Public Debt: Internal & External

Table 8.1 — Internal vs external public debt
Internal (Domestic) DebtExternal Debt
LendersDomestic banks, institutions, households (via G-Secs, small savings schemes)Foreign governments, multilateral agencies, foreign bondholders
Currency denominationTypically domestic currency (rupee)Often foreign currency (dollar, yen, etc.) — exposes to exchange rate risk
Repayment burdenA transfer within the domestic economy (from taxpayers to bondholders) — does not directly reduce national resources, though it has distributional and crowding-out effectsA real resource outflow to foreigners — genuinely reduces resources available to the domestic economy
Share of India's total public debtOverwhelmingly dominant (typically 90%+ of total public debt)A comparatively small share, reflecting cautious, calibrated external borrowing policy since the 1991 crisis lesson
Exam angle
A classic conceptual point: internal debt is a burden of redistribution (within the country, from future taxpayers to bondholders) while external debt is a burden of real resource transfer (claims by foreigners on domestic output) — this distinction is frequently tested, especially regarding why economists worry more, ceteris paribus, about rising external debt.
09 — SUSTAINABILITY

Debt Sustainability & the Debt Trap

Debt-GDP Ratio — the standard sustainability metric
Debt-GDP Ratio = (Total Public Debt / GDP) × 100
Debt Sustainability Condition (intuitive)
Public debt is considered sustainable if the rate of GDP growth (g) exceeds the effective interest rate on debt (r) — under this condition, the debt-GDP ratio tends to stabilise or fall over time even with a modest ongoing primary deficit; if r > g persistently, the debt-GDP ratio tends to explode.
Debt Trap
A situation where a government must borrow increasingly just to service (pay interest on) existing debt — new borrowing goes toward interest payments rather than productive investment, causing the debt burden to spiral upward in a self-reinforcing cycle, often necessitating painful fiscal adjustment or restructuring/default.

India's institutional response: the FRBM Act

Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003
Institutionalised numerical targets for the fiscal deficit and (originally) the revenue deficit, aiming to enforce fiscal discipline and prevent unsustainable debt accumulation — targets and timelines have been revised several times (e.g., via subsequent FRBM Review Committees) in response to changing fiscal circumstances (including a formal "escape clause" allowing deviation during exceptional circumstances such as the COVID-19 pandemic).
Exam angle
The r vs g comparison (interest rate vs growth rate) is a high-value, frequently tested sustainability condition — remember it in this simple, intuitive form even without deriving the full debt-dynamics equation.
Part D

Fiscal Federalism in India

10 — CONSTITUTIONAL FRAMEWORK

Constitutional Framework of Fiscal Federalism

Fiscal Federalism
The division of fiscal powers, responsibilities, and resources between different levels of government (Centre, States, and — under the 73rd/74th Amendments — local bodies) in a federal system.
Table 10.1 — Constitutional allocation of fiscal powers (illustrative)
ListExamples of tax/subject allocation
Union ListIncome tax (non-agricultural), customs duties, corporation tax, GST on inter-state supply (IGST)
State ListAgricultural income tax, state excise (alcohol), stamp duty, land revenue, GST on intra-state supply (SGST)
Concurrent List / SharedGST design itself is a landmark cooperative arrangement (CGST + SGST + IGST) jointly administered via the GST Council (Section 12)

A structural feature of India's fiscal federalism: revenue-raising powers are more centralised (the Centre collects a larger share of total tax revenue) while expenditure responsibilities are more decentralised (states bear larger responsibility for many welfare/development functions) — creating a built-in vertical fiscal imbalance (Section 13) that transfer mechanisms (Finance Commission, Centrally Sponsored Schemes) are designed to bridge.

11 — FINANCE COMMISSION

The Finance Commission

Finance Commission (Article 280)
A constitutional body, appointed by the President every five years (or earlier if needed), whose primary role is to recommend the distribution of the net proceeds of central taxes between the Centre and the States (vertical devolution), and the allocation of the states' share among individual states (horizontal devolution), along with principles governing grants-in-aid.

Key functions

  • Recommends the vertical devolution ratio — what share of the central divisible pool of taxes goes to states as a whole
  • Recommends the horizontal devolution formula — how that state share is split among individual states, based on criteria such as population, income distance (how far a state's per capita income is below the highest), area, forest cover, demographic performance, and tax effort
  • Recommends principles for grants-in-aid to states in need of assistance (Article 275)
  • Reviews the finances of local bodies (Panchayats and Municipalities) and recommends measures to augment their resources
Exam angle
The 15th Finance Commission (chaired by N.K. Singh, recommendations covering 2021-22 to 2025-26) notably recommended a vertical devolution share of 41% of the divisible pool to states (adjusted down slightly from the 14th Finance Commission's 42%, to account for the changed status of Jammu & Kashmir into Union Territories) — a frequently tested current-affairs-adjacent fact. Always verify the currently applicable Finance Commission and its period closer to your exam, since a new Commission's recommendations may become relevant.
12 — GST COUNCIL

GST Council & Cooperative Federalism

GST Council (Article 279A)
A constitutional body (established following the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016, which introduced GST) comprising the Union Finance Minister (Chairperson) and Finance/Revenue Ministers of every state, responsible for making recommendations on GST rates, exemptions, thresholds, and administration — the flagship modern institution of India's "cooperative federalism" approach.

Significance

  • Requires both Centre and States to cede some individual taxation autonomy in favour of a jointly-decided, harmonised indirect tax structure across the country
  • Voting structure gives the Centre one-third weightage and States collectively two-thirds weightage, with decisions requiring a three-fourths majority — designed so that neither the Centre alone nor a small subset of states can unilaterally dictate outcomes
  • Introduced a temporary GST compensation mechanism (cess-funded) to compensate states for revenue loss in GST's initial years — a recurring point of Centre-State friction, especially during the COVID-19 revenue shortfall period
Exam angle
The GST Council is frequently cited as India's most significant recent institutional innovation in fiscal federalism — remember its voting weights (Centre 1/3, States 2/3 collectively) and its basis in Article 279A, added via the 101st Amendment.
13 — FISCAL IMBALANCE

Vertical & Horizontal Fiscal Imbalance

Table 13.1 — Two distinct imbalance concepts
ConceptMeaningCorrective mechanism
Vertical Fiscal ImbalanceMismatch between the Centre's greater revenue-raising capacity and the States' greater expenditure responsibilitiesTax devolution (Finance Commission), grants-in-aid, Centrally Sponsored Schemes
Horizontal Fiscal ImbalanceDisparities among states themselves in revenue-raising capacity and developmental needs (richer vs poorer states)Finance Commission's horizontal devolution formula (weighted toward "income distance"/need-based criteria, favouring less-developed states)
The "income distance" tension
Weighting devolution formulas toward poorer states (via the "income distance" criterion) is often criticised by wealthier, better-performing states as penalising fiscal responsibility and economic success — a recurring, politically sensitive tension in Finance Commission deliberations, and a good example of the equity-vs-incentive trade-off in transfer design.
Exam angle
Don't confuse the two imbalances: vertical is Centre-vs-States-as-a-whole; horizontal is state-vs-state. Both are addressed by the Finance Commission, but through different parts of its recommendations (the vertical share vs the horizontal distribution formula).

16 — SELF-TEST

Practice MCQs

Click each question to reveal the answer & explanation.

Q1. A pure public good is characterised by: Recall
(a) Rivalry and excludability   (b) Rivalry but non-excludability   (c) Non-rivalry and non-excludability   (d) Excludability but non-rivalry

Answer: (c) — e.g., national defence, street lighting.
Q2. A congested toll road with metering technology to charge users is an example of a: Application
(a) Pure public good   (b) Club good (or, if congested, closer to a common pool resource)   (c) Common pool resource only   (d) Private good with no externality

Answer: (b) — excludable via tolling; largely non-rivalrous unless congestion sets in.
Q3. The efficient provision condition for a public good requires: Conceptual
(a) Each individual's MRS = MRT separately   (b) The sum of all individuals' MRS = MRT   (c) MRS = 0 for all individuals   (d) MRT = 0

Answer: (b) — the Samuelson condition, reflecting that all individuals consume the same unit simultaneously.
Q4. If Fiscal Deficit equals Interest Payments exactly, then: Numerical
(a) Revenue Deficit is zero   (b) Primary Deficit is zero   (c) Total Expenditure is zero   (d) The budget is in surplus

Answer: (b) — since Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments.
Q5. Government expenditure on salaries and pensions is classified as: Conceptual
(a) Capital expenditure   (b) Revenue expenditure   (c) Capital receipts   (d) Revenue receipts

Answer: (b) — routine spending that does not create an asset.
Q6. Compared to internal public debt, external public debt is generally considered more concerning because: Application
(a) It is always larger in absolute size   (b) It represents a real transfer of domestic resources to foreigners, rather than a purely internal redistribution   (c) It cannot be measured   (d) It is illegal in most countries

Answer: (b).
Q7. Public debt is generally considered sustainable when: Conceptual
(a) The interest rate on debt exceeds the GDP growth rate   (b) The GDP growth rate exceeds the interest rate on debt   (c) The debt is entirely external   (d) The primary deficit is always positive and rising

Answer: (b) — the g > r condition.
Q8. The Finance Commission is constituted under which Article of the Indian Constitution? Recall
(a) Article 275   (b) Article 280   (c) Article 279A   (d) Article 112

Answer: (b) — Article 275 deals with grants-in-aid; Article 279A established the GST Council; Article 112 requires the Annual Financial Statement (Budget).
Q9. In the GST Council's voting structure: Recall
(a) The Centre has two-thirds weightage   (b) The Centre has one-third weightage and all states collectively have two-thirds   (c) Each state and the Centre have exactly equal individual votes   (d) Only the Centre votes

Answer: (b).
Q10. "Vertical fiscal imbalance" in Indian fiscal federalism refers to: Conceptual
(a) Disparities between richer and poorer states   (b) The mismatch between the Centre's greater revenue-raising power and the States' greater expenditure responsibilities   (c) A budget deficit within a single state   (d) The gap between fiscal and revenue deficit

Answer: (b) — contrasted with "horizontal" imbalance, which is state-vs-state.
Q11. Which Act institutionalised numerical fiscal deficit targets in India? Recall
(a) GST Act, 2017   (b) FRBM Act, 2003   (c) FEMA, 1999   (d) SEBI Act, 1992

Answer: (b) — the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act.
Q12. The Pigouvian tax is a policy tool used to correct: Application
(a) A free rider problem in public goods   (b) A negative externality, by raising private cost to match social cost   (c) A vertical fiscal imbalance   (d) An unsustainable debt-GDP ratio

Answer: (b).
