MACROECONOMICS · UNIT: INCOME & OUTPUT DETERMINATION

Aggregate Demand, Aggregate Supply, Effective Demand & the Classical–Keynesian Divide

How is the equilibrium level of national income and employment determined? The Classical answer (self-correcting full employment via flexible wages/prices) versus Keynes's answer (effective demand can settle below full employment) — plus the modern AD-AS synthesis that reconciles both.

Part A

The Classical Theory of Income & Employment

01 — FOUNDATIONS

Classical Theory: Core Propositions

The Classical school (Smith, Ricardo, Marshall, Pigou — pre-1936) held that a market economy is self-regulating and automatically gravitates to full employment, with no need for government intervention.

  • Economy always tends toward full-employment equilibrium in the long run
  • Say's Law: "Supply creates its own demand" — general overproduction (a demand shortfall for the economy as a whole) is impossible
  • Perfectly flexible wages and prices ensure markets (labour, goods, loanable funds) always clear
  • Laissez-faire — minimal government intervention is both necessary and sufficient
  • Money is neutral — it affects only the price level, not real output/employment (Classical Dichotomy)
02 — SAY'S LAW

Say's Law & Automatic Full Employment

Say's Law of Markets
"Supply creates its own demand" — the very act of producing goods generates income (wages, rent, interest, profit) exactly equal in value to the goods produced, which is then spent, so aggregate demand automatically equals aggregate supply. General glut (overproduction) is logically impossible.

The three markets that ensure automatic adjustment

Table 2.1 — Classical self-adjusting mechanisms
MarketAdjusting price/variableEnsures
Labour marketMoney wage rate (W)Flexible wages clear the labour market → full employment (any unemployment is purely voluntary or frictional)
Loanable funds marketRate of interest (r)Interest rate adjusts to equate planned Saving (S) with planned Investment (I) — ensures any leakage from spending (saving) is automatically re-injected (investment)
Goods marketGeneral price level (P)Flexible prices clear the goods market, ensuring output = demand at full employment
Classical Saving-Investment equality
S(r) = I(r)   — both S and I are functions of the interest rate; r adjusts until they are equal, automatically, at full-employment income
Exam angle
In the Classical model, the rate of interest is a real phenomenon determined in the loanable funds (S-I) market — this is a key point of departure from Keynes, who instead made interest a monetary phenomenon (liquidity preference: demand and supply of money).
03 — MONEY IN THE CLASSICAL MODEL

The Classical Dichotomy & Quantity Theory of Money

Classical Dichotomy
The idea that real variables (output, employment, relative prices, the real interest rate) are determined entirely in the real sector (production functions, labour market), completely independent of the monetary sector, which determines only the nominal price level.
Quantity Theory of Money (Fisher's Equation of Exchange)
M·V = P·Y   (M = money supply, V = velocity of circulation, P = price level, Y = real output)

With V and Y both assumed constant/fixed at full employment (Classical view), any change in M translates proportionately into a change in P alone — this is the doctrine of the neutrality of money: money is only a "veil" over the real economy.

Exam angle
"Classical economists believed money is neutral" is a frequently tested True/False — it's True, and directly implies that monetary policy cannot affect real output/employment in the Classical model, only the price level.
Part B

The Keynesian Revolution

04 — THE CRITIQUE

Keynes's Critique of Classical Theory (The General Theory, 1936)

Writing against the backdrop of the Great Depression (mass, prolonged unemployment despite Classical predictions of self-correction), Keynes attacked the Classical model at its foundations.

Table 4.1 — Point-by-point critique
Classical claimKeynes's counter-argument
Say's Law: supply creates its own demandRejected — income earned need not all be spent; saving and investment decisions are made by different people for different reasons, so a demand shortfall (general glut) is entirely possible
Wages are perfectly flexible downwardWages are "sticky" downward (rigid) — due to labour contracts, social norms, minimum wage laws, union resistance — so labour markets don't clear automatically
Interest rate equates S and IInterest is a monetary phenomenon (determined by liquidity preference — demand and supply of money), not automatically equilibrating S and I; S and I are equilibrated instead by changes in the level of income (Y)
Full employment is the "normal" stateUnderemployment equilibrium is possible and can persist — the economy can settle in equilibrium at an income level well below full employment, with no automatic forces pushing it to Yf
Unemployment is only voluntary/frictionalIntroduced the idea of involuntary unemployment — workers willing to work at the prevailing wage but unable to find jobs
05 — CENTRAL PRINCIPLE

The Effective Demand Principle

Effective Demand
The point of Aggregate Demand (AD) at which it equals Aggregate Supply (AS) — the level of expected sales revenue that actually induces entrepreneurs to produce and employ at a given level of output. Employment and income are determined at exactly this point.

Aggregate Demand Price (D) and Aggregate Supply Price (Z)

  • Aggregate Supply Price (Z): the minimum total sale-proceeds entrepreneurs must expect to receive to make it worthwhile to offer a given level of employment (covers cost of production, including normal profit) — rises with employment/output
  • Aggregate Demand Price (D): the total sale-proceeds entrepreneurs actually expect to receive from selling the output produced at a given level of employment — also generally rises with employment/output, but more slowly at high income (due to the consumption function)
Effective Demand Point
Effective Demand occurs where   D = Z   — entrepreneurs have no incentive to expand or contract employment further at that point.
Exam angle
Effective Demand is the core Keynesian principle underlying the entire theory of income/employment determination — it directly implies that equilibrium output can settle below full employment, with no automatic tendency to move to Nf, contradicting Say's Law.
06 — CONSUMPTION

The Consumption Function

Consumption Function
C = f(Y) — the functional relationship between the level of national income (Y) and aggregate consumption expenditure (C). Keynes's simplest (linear) form:
Linear Consumption Function
C = C̄ + cY   (C̄ = autonomous consumption, i.e. consumption even at zero income; c = Marginal Propensity to Consume, 0 < c < 1)
Keynes's "Fundamental Psychological Law"
As income rises, consumption also rises, but by a smaller amount than the rise in income — i.e., people tend to save a larger fraction of any income increase. This implies 0 < MPC < 1.
Table 6.1 — APC, MPC and their relationship
ConceptFormulaBehaviour as Y rises
Average Propensity to Consume (APC)APC = C/YFalls (since some consumption is autonomous, fixed regardless of Y)
Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)MPC = ΔC/ΔYAssumed constant in the simple linear model (=c), typically 0<MPC<1
Average Propensity to Save (APS)APS = S/Y = 1 − APCRises as income rises
Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)MPS = ΔS/ΔY = 1 − MPCConstant (=1−c) in the simple linear model
07 — SAVING & INVESTMENT

Saving & Investment Functions

Saving Function (derived from consumption function)
S = Y − C = Y − (C̄ + cY) = −C̄ + (1−c)Y
Investment (Keynesian view)
Investment is largely treated as autonomous (Ī) in the simple model — determined by business expectations, the Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC), and the rate of interest — rather than by current income.
Marginal Efficiency of Capital (MEC)
The expected rate of return on an additional unit of capital investment. Investment is undertaken up to the point where MEC = rate of interest (r).

In more advanced models, investment also has an induced component (rising with income, via the "accelerator" effect) — but the simple Keynesian cross typically treats I as autonomous (I = Ī) for tractability.

08 — THE KEYNESIAN CROSS

The 45° Diagram (Keynesian Cross)

Equilibrium condition (two-sector economy)
Y = C + I   (Aggregate Expenditure = Aggregate Income)   ⟺   S = I

Deflationary Gap vs Inflationary Gap

Table 8.1 — The two disequilibrium "gaps"
GapMeaningCorresponding policy response
Deflationary GapAggregate Demand at full employment income falls short of the AS needed for full employment — Ye < YfExpansionary fiscal/monetary policy to boost AD (↑G, ↓T)
Inflationary GapAggregate Demand at full employment income exceeds full-employment output capacity — demand-pull pressure on pricesContractionary fiscal/monetary policy to curb AD (↓G, ↑T)
09 — EXTENDING THE MODEL

Two-, Three- and Four-Sector Equilibrium

Table 9.1 — Equilibrium conditions across models
ModelEquilibrium (Income = Expenditure)Equilibrium (Leakages = Injections)
Two-sectorY = C + IS = I
Three-sector (+ Govt)Y = C + I + GS + T = I + G

Exam angle
The "leakages = injections" version is an equivalent, often more intuitive, way to state Keynesian equilibrium — leakages (S, T, M) withdraw purchasing power from the circular flow, while injections (I, G, X) inject it back in; equilibrium requires the two to balance exactly.
10 — THE MULTIPLIER

The Investment (Keynesian) Multiplier

Multiplier (k)
The ratio of the change in equilibrium income to the initial change in autonomous expenditure (e.g., investment) that caused it. Captures the "ripple effect": one person's spending is another's income, which is partly re-spent, and so on.
Formula (two-sector, simple model)
k = ΔY/ΔI = 1/(1−MPC) = 1/MPS

Exam angle
Higher MPC (lower MPS) ⇒ larger multiplier (less leaked into saving at each round). Adding tax and import leakages always shrinks the multiplier relative to the simple two-sector case — a very common numerical MCQ setup.
11 — A KEYNESIAN PARADOX

Paradox of Thrift

Paradox of Thrift
An attempt by all individuals in the economy to save more (at any given income) can, paradoxically, lead to a fall in aggregate income and, in the simple closed model, even leave aggregate saving unchanged — even though saving more is individually rational.

Mechanism: if households collectively try to save more, consumption spending (C) falls → aggregate demand falls → firms cut production and lay off workers → national income falls → since income has fallen, saving may end up no higher (or even lower) than before, despite everyone's intention to save more.

Exam angle
A "fallacy of composition" — what is prudent for one individual (saving more) is not necessarily good for the economy as a whole if generalised, when investment doesn't rise correspondingly. Directly contradicts the Classical presumption that more saving is unambiguously good for capital formation.
Part C

The Modern AD-AS Framework

12 — AGGREGATE DEMAND

The Aggregate Demand (AD) Curve

Aggregate Demand (modern AD-AS sense)
The total quantity of domestically produced goods and services that all sectors of the economy (households, firms, government, foreign sector) are willing to purchase at each possible price level, holding other factors constant.
Composition
AD = C + I + G + (X − M)

Why does the AD curve slope downward (in P–Y space)?

  • Real balance (wealth) effect — a higher price level reduces the real value of money holdings, reducing consumption
  • Interest rate effect — a higher price level raises money demand, pushing up interest rates, which discourages investment
  • Net export (exchange rate) effect — a higher domestic price level makes exports relatively costlier and imports cheaper, reducing net exports
Do not confuse
This AD curve (price level vs total real output) is conceptually distinct from a single-market demand curve (own price vs quantity of one good) — the downward slope here comes from wealth/interest-rate/exchange-rate effects, not from a substitution effect between goods.

17 — SELF-TEST

Practice MCQs

Click each question to reveal the answer & explanation.

Q1. Say's Law states that: Recall
(a) Demand creates its own supply   (b) Supply creates its own demand   (c) Government spending is always necessary   (d) Wages are always rigid

Answer: (b) — the foundational Classical proposition ruling out general overproduction.
Q2. In the Classical model, the rate of interest is determined in the: Conceptual
(a) Money market (liquidity preference)   (b) Loanable funds market, equating planned saving and investment   (c) Labour market   (d) Foreign exchange market

Answer: (b) — a real, not monetary, phenomenon in the Classical framework.
Q3. Keynes's "Effective Demand" is the point where: Recall
(a) Consumption equals Investment   (b) Aggregate Demand Price equals Aggregate Supply Price   (c) Saving equals zero   (d) Money supply equals money demand

Answer: (b) — D=Z determines the equilibrium level of employment and output.
Q4. According to Keynes's "Fundamental Psychological Law of Consumption": Conceptual
(a) Consumption rises by more than income   (b) Consumption rises, but by less than the rise in income, as income increases   (c) Consumption is unrelated to income   (d) Consumption always exceeds income

Answer: (b) — implying 0 < MPC < 1.
Q5. If MPC = 0.75, the value of the simple (two-sector) multiplier is: Numerical
(a) 0.75   (b) 1.75   (c) 4   (d) 3

Answer: (c) — k = 1/(1−0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4.
Q6. Introducing a marginal propensity to import (MPM) into the multiplier formula will: Application
(a) Increase the size of the multiplier   (b) Decrease the size of the multiplier (an added leakage)   (c) Have no effect   (d) Make the multiplier negative

Answer: (b) — k = 1/(1−MPC+MPM); a higher MPM raises the denominator, shrinking k.
Q7. The "Paradox of Thrift" suggests that an economy-wide increase in the desire to save can lead to: Conceptual
(a) A proportionate increase in aggregate saving   (b) A fall in national income, potentially leaving aggregate saving unchanged   (c) An automatic rise in investment   (d) No change in income or saving

Answer: (b).
Q8. The Classical Aggregate Supply curve is: Recall
(a) Horizontal   (b) Upward-sloping throughout   (c) Vertical, at the full-employment level of output   (d) Downward-sloping

Answer: (c) — reflecting the assumption that output is fixed at its full-employment (potential) level regardless of the price level.
Q9. In the Keynesian (flat portion of the) AS curve, an increase in Aggregate Demand primarily causes: Application
(a) A rise in the price level only   (b) A rise in real output/employment, with little or no rise in the price level   (c) A fall in output   (d) No change in either output or price

Answer: (b) — because there is spare (unemployed) capacity in this range.
Q10. Which of the following is one of the three "real balance / wealth, interest-rate, and net-export" reasons the AD curve slopes downward? Assertion-set
I. A higher price level reduces the real value of money holdings, reducing consumption
II. A higher price level raises money demand, raising interest rates and discouraging investment
III. A higher price level makes exports relatively cheaper, boosting net exports
IV. A higher price level makes domestic goods relatively costlier abroad, reducing net exports

(a) I, II & III   (b) I, II & IV   (c) II & III only   (d) All four

Answer: (b) — Statement III has the direction reversed: a higher domestic price level makes exports relatively costlier, not cheaper, reducing (not boosting) net exports.
Q11. "Money is neutral" is a proposition associated with: Recall
(a) Keynesian theory   (b) Classical theory   (c) Paradox of Thrift   (d) Effective Demand Principle

Answer: (b) — under the Classical Dichotomy, money affects only nominal variables (P), never real output/employment.
Q12. A "deflationary gap" exists when: Conceptual
(a) Aggregate Demand at full-employment income exceeds full-employment output   (b) Aggregate Demand at full-employment income falls short of the level needed for full employment   (c) Prices are falling rapidly   (d) The multiplier equals zero

Answer: (b) — the mirror image of an inflationary gap.
