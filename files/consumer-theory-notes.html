01 — ORIENTATION

Meaning & the Three Approaches

Consumer behaviour theory asks one question: how does a rational, utility-maximising consumer allocate a limited income across goods to get the most satisfaction? Three successive schools answered it — each fixing a weakness of the one before.

Approach 1 — Cardinal Utility
Alfred Marshall (1890). Utility is measurable in cardinal numbers (units called "utils"). Consumer equalises marginal utility per rupee across goods.
Approach 2 — Ordinal Utility (Indifference Curves)
Hicks & Allen (1934). Utility cannot be measured, only ranked/ordered ("I prefer A to B"). Consumer reaches tangency of budget line and indifference curve.
Approach 3 — Revealed Preference Theory
Paul Samuelson (1938), refined by Houthakker. Drops utility altogether — infers preferences purely from observed market choices, assuming only behavioural consistency.

02 — FOUNDATION

Theory of Demand

Demand (economic sense)
Demand = Desire + Ability to pay + Willingness to pay, at a given price, in a given time period. Mere "wants" are not demand.

Determinants of demand

  • Price of the good itself (Px)
  • Prices of related goods — substitutes (Ps) and complements (Pc)
  • Income of the consumer (Y)
  • Tastes & preferences (T)
  • Consumer's expectations of future prices
  • Number of consumers in the market (for market demand)
Generalised Demand Function
Qdx = f(Px, Ps, Pc, Y, T, E)

The Law of Demand

Statement: Other things remaining constant (ceteris paribus), the quantity demanded of a good varies inversely with its price.

Why does the demand curve slope downward?

  • Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility — each extra unit gives less satisfaction, so consumer pays less for it
  • Income effect — a price fall raises real income, so more is bought
  • Substitution effect — the good becomes relatively cheaper than substitutes
  • New buyers enter the market at a lower price
  • Multiple uses of a commodity get activated as price falls
Table 2.1 — Movement vs. Shift
Movement along the curveShift of the curve
CauseChange in own priceChange in any other determinant (income, tastes, related prices)
Terminology"Change / Extension & Contraction in Quantity Demanded""Change / Increase & Decrease in Demand"
Curve itselfSame curve, different pointEntirely new curve
Exceptions to the Law of Demand (upward-sloping demand)
  • Giffen goods — inferior goods with a very high proportion of income spent on them (Sir Robert Giffen; classic: bread among poor 19th-century English labourers)
  • Veblen / conspicuous consumption goods — diamonds, luxury cars; demand rises with price as a status signal
  • Speculative goods — shares, real estate: price rise triggers expectation of further rise → more buying
  • Necessities — life-saving drugs, salt: near-zero elasticity, quantity barely changes with price
  • Ignorance / fashion effect — price mistaken as a proxy for quality
Exam angle
CUET PG loves testing the difference between "change in demand" (shift) vs "change in quantity demanded" (movement) with assertion–reason or match-the-following formats. Also watch for Giffen vs Veblen distinction — Giffen is about inferior necessities among the poor; Veblen is about luxury/status goods.
03 — CARDINAL APPROACH

Cardinal Utility Analysis (Marshall)

Assumptions

  • Utility is cardinally measurable (in "utils"), like weight or height
  • Marginal utility of money remains constant (money itself is not subject to diminishing utility over the relevant range)
  • Utility is additive: TU = U1 + U2 + ... + Un (independent utilities)
  • Consumer is rational and aims to maximise total utility
  • Diminishing marginal utility holds

Total Utility (TU) & Marginal Utility (MU)

Definitions
TUn = U1 + U2 + ... + Un   |   MUn = TUn − TUn−1   |   MU = ΔTU / ΔQ

Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility (LDMU)

Statement (Gossen's First Law)
As a consumer takes successive units of a commodity, the marginal utility derived from each additional unit goes on diminishing, other things (income, tastes, no time gap) remaining constant.

Assumptions of LDMU

  • Units of the good are standard/identical
  • Consumption is continuous — no long time gap between units
  • Consumer's income, taste, and habits stay unchanged
  • Rationality of the consumer

Exceptions

  • Money — its marginal utility is assumed constant / diminishes very slowly
  • Hobbies/collections — e.g., stamp or coin collecting (MU may rise with more units, up to a point)
  • First few units of alcohol/addictive goods (in some formulations MU initially rises)

Law of Equi-Marginal Utility (Gossen's Second Law / Law of Substitution)

Statement
A consumer with a fixed income allocates it across goods such that the marginal utility per rupee spent is equal across all goods, and equal to the marginal utility of money.
Condition for consumer's equilibrium (cardinal)
MUx / Px  =  MUy / Py  =  ...  =  MUm (marginal utility of money)
subject to budget constraint:   Px·X + Py·Y = M (income)

Intuition: if MUx/Px > MUy/Py, the consumer gets more "utility per rupee" from X, so she shifts spending toward X. As she buys more X, MUx falls (LDMU); as she buys less Y, MUy rises — until the ratios equalise.

Deriving the demand curve (Marshallian method)

Since MUx itself declines as more X is consumed (LDMU), and equilibrium requires MUx = Px × MUm (with MUm constant), a fall in Px requires a higher MUx to restore equality — which only happens at a lower quantity of X consumed being pushed back up... in short: lower Px ⇒ consumer buys more X until MUx falls enough to match. Thus the individual's MU curve is, in effect, the demand curve (rescaled by MUm).

Criticisms of the Cardinal Approach

  • Utility is psychological and cannot really be measured in cardinal numbers — utils are fictitious units
  • Assumes constant marginal utility of money — unrealistic, since money is also a good subject to diminishing utility
  • Assumes independent utilities (additivity) — ignores that goods are often complements/substitutes whose utilities are interdependent
  • Introspective method is unscientific
  • Cannot explain consumer behaviour with two or more goods analysed simultaneously as elegantly as IC analysis
04 — ORDINAL APPROACH

Indifference Curve (IC) Analysis

Developed by J.R. Hicks and R.G.D. Allen (1934) to overcome cardinal measurability. Utility only needs to be ranked, not measured.

Indifference Curve
The locus of all combinations of two goods (X, Y) that give the consumer the same level of total satisfaction, such that they are indifferent between any two points on it.
Indifference Map
A family/set of indifference curves, each representing a different (higher or lower) level of satisfaction. Curves farther from the origin represent higher utility.

Assumptions of IC analysis

  • Rationality — consumer maximises satisfaction
  • Ordinal utility — can rank bundles, not measure exact utility
  • Consistency — if A ≻ B, then B cannot ≻ A in the same period
  • Transitivity — if A ≻ B and B ≻ C, then A ≻ C
  • Non-satiety — more of a good is always preferred to less (monotonicity)
  • Diminishing MRS — willingness to give up Y for X declines as X increases
  • Two-commodity world (for graphical tractability)

Properties of Indifference Curves

Table 4.1 — The five core properties
#PropertyReasoning
1Negatively sloped (downward from left to right)To keep utility constant, more of X must be compensated by less of Y
2Convex to the originFollows diminishing Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)
3Higher IC ⇒ higher satisfactionNon-satiety — a curve farther from origin has more of both/either good
4Two ICs can never intersectIntersection would violate transitivity (would imply equal & unequal satisfaction simultaneously)
5Do not touch either axisAssumes both goods are desired in positive quantities

Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)

Definition
MRSxy = − ΔY / ΔX   (along a given IC)  =  the amount of Y the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of X, keeping satisfaction constant.
Also equals the (absolute) slope of the IC at that point, and MRSxy = MUx / MUy.

Diminishing MRS: as the consumer acquires more of X (and gives up Y), each extra unit of X is valued less relative to Y — so the amount of Y sacrificed for each successive unit of X falls. This diminishing MRS is what makes the IC convex to the origin.

The Budget Line (Price Line)

Budget constraint
Px·X + Py·Y = M   ⟹   Y = M/Py − (Px/Py)·X
Slope of budget line = − Px/Py
  • Income change (prices constant) → budget line shifts parallel outward (income ↑) or inward (income ↓)
  • Price change of one good → budget line pivots/rotates from the intercept of the good whose price changed
05 — TANGENCY

Consumer's Equilibrium under IC Analysis

Equilibrium (tangency) condition
MRSxy = Px / Py    — the slope of the IC equals the slope of the budget line, at the highest attainable IC.

Two conditions must both hold

  1. First-order: the budget line must be tangent to an indifference curve, i.e. MRSxy = Px/Py
  2. Second-order: the IC must be convex to the origin at that point (i.e., MRS is diminishing) — this ensures it's a genuine maximum, not a minimum
Exam angle
A very common CUET PG MCQ pattern: "At the point of consumer's equilibrium..." followed by 4 statements to mark true/false — e.g. "MRS = price ratio" (True), "IC is concave at that point" (False — must be convex), "It is the point of maximum satisfaction for given income" (True).
06 — DECOMPOSING THE PRICE EFFECT

Income Effect, Substitution Effect & Price Effect

Substitution Effect (SE)
The change in quantity demanded of X due purely to a change in its relative price, with real income (utility) held constant. Always negative (price ↓ ⇒ quantity ↑) — works in the same direction for all goods.
Income Effect (IE)
The change in quantity demanded due to the change in real income caused by the price change, with relative prices held constant. Sign depends on whether the good is normal or inferior.
Decomposition
Price Effect (PE) = Substitution Effect (SE) + Income Effect (IE)

Slutsky vs Hicksian decomposition

07 — DERIVED CURVES

ICC, PCC & Engel Curve

Income Consumption Curve (ICC) & the Engel Curve

ICC
The locus of consumer equilibrium points traced out as income changes, holding prices constant (i.e., budget line shifts parallel outward at successive income levels).
Engel Curve
Derived from the ICC — plots quantity of a good demanded against income, prices held constant. Named after statistician Ernst Engel.
Table 7.1 — Engel curve shapes & income elasticity
Good typeIncome elasticity (EY)Engel curve shape
Necessity0 < EY < 1Rises, but flattens (less than proportionate rise)
LuxuryEY > 1Rises steeply (more than proportionate)
Inferior goodEY < 0Rises then bends backward (negatively sloped beyond a point)
Giffen goodEY < 0 (strongly)Backward-bending; also gives upward-sloping demand curve

Price Consumption Curve (PCC) & deriving the Demand Curve

PCC
The locus of consumer equilibrium points traced out as the price of one good (X) changes, income and price of Y held constant (budget line pivots around the Y-intercept).

Plotting the successive equilibrium quantities of X (from the PCC) against the corresponding prices of X gives the individual demand curve for X — this is the graphical bridge from IC theory to the ordinary demand curve.

08 — SPECIAL CASE

The Giffen Paradox

Giffen good
Named after Sir Robert Giffen. A good for which quantity demanded falls when its price falls (and rises when price rises) — an upward-sloping demand curve.

Conditions for a Giffen good

  • Must be an inferior good (negative income effect)
  • The good must take up a large share of the consumer's budget
  • No close substitutes available
  • The negative income effect must be strong enough to outweigh the (always positive-for-quantity) substitution effect
Exam angle
Classic trap: "All Giffen goods are inferior goods, but not all inferior goods are Giffen goods." This statement is TRUE — inferiority is necessary but not sufficient; the income effect must also dominate the substitution effect.
09 — BEHAVIOURIST APPROACH

Revealed Preference Theory (Samuelson)

Paul Samuelson (1938) argued we don't need to assume anything about internal "utility" or "indifference" at all — we can derive the whole theory of demand purely from observed choices in the market.

Assumptions

  • Rationality — the consumer chooses the best affordable bundle
  • Consistency — if bundle A is chosen over affordable B, the consumer will never choose B over A when both are affordable again
  • Transitivity (needed for the Strong Axiom)
  • No consumer's tastes change during the period under observation
  • Goods are homogeneous / divisible
"Revealed Preferred"
If bundle A is chosen when bundle B was also affordable (within the same budget set), then A is said to be "revealed preferred" to B.

Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference (WARP)

Statement
If A is revealed preferred to B (A chosen when B was affordable), then B can never be revealed preferred to A (i.e., B must never be chosen over A) in any situation where A is also affordable.

Strong Axiom of Revealed Preference (SARP)

Statement (Houthakker, 1950)
Extends WARP to chains: if A is revealed preferred to B, and B is revealed preferred to C, then A must be revealed preferred to C directly (transitivity of revealed preference across any number of bundles).

Deriving the demand curve via Revealed Preference

Using only WARP/SARP and two successive budget lines (before and after a price change, with an income adjustment to keep real purchasing power roughly constant — a Slutsky-style compensation), Samuelson showed the substitution effect must always be negative: a fall in relative price of X, with income compensated, must lead to more X being chosen — deriving the law of demand without ever invoking indifference curves or utility.

Superiority of Revealed Preference over IC approach

  • Based entirely on observable behaviour — no unmeasurable psychological concepts (utility, indifference)
  • Derives the law of demand from fewer, weaker assumptions
  • Provides an empirically testable foundation for demand theory

Limitations

  • Still assumes consistency and transitivity of choices, which may not always hold in reality (behavioural economics critiques)
  • Cannot handle more than 2 changes/goods as intuitively in diagrams
  • Doesn't explain why a bundle is preferred, only that it was chosen
10 — WELFARE MEASURE

Consumer Surplus

Marshallian Consumer Surplus
The excess of what a consumer is willing to pay for a given quantity of a good over what they actually pay (at the market price).
Formula
CS = Total utility (in money terms) of the good − Total actual expenditure
CS = Σ(willingness to pay per unit) − (P × Q)

Assumptions behind the Marshallian measure

  • Marginal utility of money remains constant (same weakness as cardinal utility theory)
  • Utility is cardinally measurable in money terms
  • No substitutes affect the specific good's demand curve independently

Hicks's critique & alternative measures

J.R. Hicks criticised Marshall's CS for relying on a constant marginal utility of money — invalid once we allow ordinal utility and income effects. Hicks proposed two rigorous, IC-based welfare measures:

Table 10.1 — Hicksian welfare measures
MeasureDefinitionReference indifference curve
Compensating Variation (CV)The amount of money that must be taken away from (or given to) the consumer after a price fall (rise) to bring them back to their original utility levelOriginal IC
Equivalent Variation (EV)The amount of money that would have to be given to (or taken from) the consumer instead of the price change, to leave them as well off as the price change wouldNew IC
Exam angle
For a normal good, the standard ranking is: EV < Marshallian CS < CV (for a price fall). CUET PG occasionally tests this ordering directly, or asks which measure uses the "old" vs "new" price/utility level as reference.

Applications of consumer surplus

  • Evaluating the welfare loss/gain from taxation or subsidy
  • Cost-benefit analysis of public projects (e.g., value of a new road, bridge)
  • Measuring gains from trade / opening to international trade
  • Price discrimination analysis (monopolist extracting consumer surplus)

13 — SELF-TEST

Practice MCQs

Click each question to reveal the answer & explanation. Multi-statement format mirrors CUET PG style.

Q1. The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility was first stated by: Recall
(a) Marshall   (b) Gossen   (c) Hicks   (d) Samuelson

Answer: (b) Gossen — known as Gossen's First Law; Marshall later popularised and formalised it in English economics.
Q2. At consumer's equilibrium under indifference curve analysis, which of the following is/are true? Assertion-set
I. MRSxy = Px/Py
II. The indifference curve is convex to the origin at that point
III. The budget line lies entirely above the indifference curve
IV. It represents the highest attainable satisfaction for the given budget

(a) I & II only   (b) I, II & IV only   (c) All four   (d) II & III only

Answer: (b) — Statement III is false: the budget line is tangent to the IC at equilibrium, not entirely above it (that would mean the bundle is unaffordable).
Q3. Which good has an income elasticity of demand that is negative? Application
(a) Normal good   (b) Luxury good   (c) Inferior good   (d) Necessity

Answer: (c) Inferior good — quantity demanded falls as income rises, giving EY < 0.
Q4. The Weak Axiom of Revealed Preference states that: Conceptual
(a) Preferences must be transitive across any chain of bundles
(b) If A is chosen over affordable B, B can never be chosen over A when A is affordable
(c) Utility must be cardinally measurable
(d) Indifference curves must be convex

Answer: (b) — (a) describes the Strong Axiom (SARP), which extends WARP to chains via transitivity.
Q5. In the Hicksian decomposition of a price fall, the movement from the original equilibrium to the point on the same indifference curve (with a compensated budget line) represents: Diagram-based
(a) Income effect   (b) Substitution effect   (c) Price effect   (d) Total effect

Answer: (b) Substitution effect — by construction, this movement stays on the same IC (constant utility), isolating the pure relative-price response.
Q6. Which of the following best orders the Hicksian and Marshallian welfare measures for a price fall in a normal good? Advanced
(a) CV < CS < EV   (b) EV < CS < CV   (c) CS < EV < CV   (d) All are equal

Answer: (b) — Equivalent Variation is smallest, Marshallian Consumer Surplus lies in between, Compensating Variation is largest, for a price fall in a normal good.
Q7. Two indifference curves can never intersect because it would violate: Recall
(a) Non-satiety   (b) Convexity   (c) Transitivity of preferences   (d) Diminishing MRS

Answer: (c) — an intersection would imply the same bundle yields both equal and unequal satisfaction relative to different curves, breaking consistency/transitivity.
Q8. A Giffen good must necessarily be: Conceptual
(a) A luxury good   (b) An inferior good with a dominant negative income effect   (c) A good with many substitutes   (d) A normal good

Answer: (b) — inferiority is necessary but not sufficient; the negative income effect must outweigh the positive substitution effect.
Q9. The Engel curve for an inferior good typically: Diagram-based
(a) Rises continuously   (b) Is a straight line through the origin   (c) Rises then bends backward (negative slope) beyond a threshold income   (d) Is vertical

Answer: (c) — as income keeps rising, consumers switch away from the inferior good toward superior substitutes.
Q10. Which theory of consumer behaviour relies least on unobservable psychological concepts? Comparative
(a) Cardinal utility approach   (b) Ordinal/IC approach   (c) Revealed preference theory   (d) None of these

Answer: (c) — RPT is built entirely from observed market choices, making it the most empirically grounded/behaviourist approach.
