INDIAN ECONOMY · FROM COLONY TO PLANNING TO LIBERALIZATION

Changes & Continuities, Government and Fiscal Policy, and the New Economic Policy

What did 1947 actually change — and what colonial-era structures persisted? How did the planning-era state build a mixed economy and manage fiscal policy for four decades? And why, in 1991, did India abandon that model for Liberalization, Privatization and Globalization (LPG)? A single connected arc from empire to reform.

Part A

Colonial to Independent India: Changes & Continuities

01 — RUPTURE

What Changed at Independence (1947)

  • Political sovereignty — economic policy could now, for the first time, be set with Indian development as the explicit primary objective, not subordinated to metropolitan (British) interests
  • End of the "Drain" — Home Charges and the structural, compulsory outward transfer of resources to Britain ceased
  • Adoption of centralised economic planning — the Planning Commission (1950) and the Five Year Plans introduced a wholly new instrument of state-directed development absent under colonial rule
  • State-led industrialization strategy — a deliberate policy choice (influenced by the Soviet model and the Mahalanobis strategy) to build heavy industry and a large public sector, a sharp departure from colonial-era neglect of Indian capital-goods industry
  • Abolition of intermediary land tenures — zamindari abolition acts (1950s) formally ended the zamindari system, aiming to establish direct state-cultivator relations (though implementation and effectiveness varied significantly by state)
  • New currency/central banking framework oriented toward domestic developmental priorities rather than London-favourable exchange stability
02 — CONTINUITY

What Continued: Structural Legacies of Colonialism

Independence was a political rupture, not an instant economic one
Despite the change in political control, many structural features of the colonial economy persisted well into the independent period, shaping the starting conditions and constraints planners had to work with.
Table 2.1 — Persisting colonial-era structures
LegacyHow it persisted
Agrarian structureFragmented, unequal landholding patterns; tenancy insecurity persisted in many regions despite land reform legislation, due to weak implementation and loopholes
Occupational structureOverwhelming majority of the workforce remained in agriculture (see the Occupational Structure topic notes) — the colonial-era imbalance between output and employment structure did not disappear overnight
Low savings & capital baseDecades of drain and limited reinvestment left India with very low initial capital stock and a low savings rate (~10% of GDP at Independence) to fund development
Narrow industrial baseThe limited, colonially-constrained industrial structure (Section 12, prior notes) — weak capital-goods sector, import-dependent for machinery — remained the starting point
Regional industrial concentrationBombay and Calcutta/Bengal (and a few other pockets) remained India's dominant industrial centres, reflecting colonial-era location patterns
Administrative & legal institutionsThe colonial civil service (reformed into the Indian Administrative Service), legal system, and much administrative machinery were retained rather than rebuilt from scratch
Trade orientationContinued significant trade dependence on former colonial-era patterns/partners in the early independence decades, only gradually diversifying
Exam angle
This "changes vs continuities" framing is a favourite essay-style prompt — the expected answer explicitly separates political/institutional changes (planning, sovereignty, end of the drain) from persisting structural/material conditions (low savings, agrarian backwardness, narrow industrial base) that planning had to address as inherited constraints, not choices.
Part B

Government & Fiscal Policy: The Planning Era (1950-1991)

03 — THE MODEL

The Mixed Economy & Planning Framework

Mixed Economy
India's post-Independence development strategy combined state ownership/control of "commanding heights" industries (heavy industry, infrastructure, banking) with a continuing private sector (especially in consumer goods, agriculture), guided by centralized indicative/directive planning — distinct from both pure capitalism and full Soviet-style central planning.
  • Planning Commission established in 1950; first Five Year Plan launched 1951-56
  • Mahalanobis Strategy (Second Plan, 1956-61) — prioritised rapid build-up of basic and heavy industry (capital goods), on the reasoning that a strong capital-goods base was the key to long-run self-sufficient industrial growth, even at the cost of slower near-term consumer-goods growth
  • Emphasis on import substitution — building domestic capacity to replace imports, protected by high tariffs and quantitative restrictions
  • Extensive system of industrial licensing ("Licence-Permit-Quota Raj") governing what could be produced, by whom, in what quantity
Exam angle
The Mahalanobis strategy is frequently tested for its logic: prioritising Department-I (capital/producer goods) industries over Department-II (consumer goods) was meant to build a self-reliant industrial base immune to foreign exchange constraints — critics later argued this contributed to chronic consumer-goods shortages and inefficiency.
04 — INDUSTRIAL POLICY

Industrial Policy Resolutions

Table 4.1 — Key Industrial Policy Resolutions before 1991
ResolutionKey provisions
Industrial Policy Resolution, 1948First post-Independence statement; divided industry into categories — state monopoly (e.g., defence, atomic energy), mixed public-private, and private sector with state regulation — an early, still moderate articulation of the mixed-economy approach
Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956The definitive statement of the mixed-economy/socialistic pattern — classified industries into three schedules: Schedule A (exclusive state responsibility — e.g., railways, atomic energy, heavy industry), Schedule B (state-plus-private, state taking the lead), Schedule C (left to the private sector, subject to regulation). Formed the basis of India's industrial structure for decades
Industrial Policy Statement, 1977 & 1980Some emphasis shifts (e.g., 1977's focus on small-scale/cottage industry), but the overall licensing-based, state-dominated framework remained largely intact
05 — FISCAL POLICY

Fiscal Policy under Planning

Revenue side

  • Heavy reliance on indirect taxes (excise, customs) rather than direct taxes for most of the period — a continuity of colonial-era regressivity in some respects, though income tax coverage gradually expanded
  • Very high marginal income tax rates at various points (reaching over 90% combined with surcharges by the early 1970s) — intended to reduce inequality but widely criticised for encouraging tax evasion and discouraging investment/entrepreneurship
  • Progressive expansion of the tax base and administrative capacity over the decades, but the system remained complex and narrow (a small proportion of the population within the income tax net)

Expenditure side

  • Large and growing Plan expenditure — funding public sector enterprises, infrastructure, agricultural investment (irrigation especially post-Green Revolution)
  • Significant and rising subsidies (food, fertiliser, and later petroleum) becoming a persistent fiscal burden
  • Growth of the fiscal deficit over time, especially from the 1980s, as expenditure commitments outpaced revenue mobilisation — a key structural weakness feeding into the 1991 crisis (Section 8)
Fiscal Deficit
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts (excluding borrowings)   — i.e., the government's total borrowing requirement
06 — PUBLIC SECTOR

The Public Sector & "Commanding Heights"

"Commanding Heights" of the Economy
A phrase (borrowed from Lenin, popularised in the Indian context by Nehru) referring to the strategic, capital-intensive core sectors — steel, heavy machinery, power, mining, banking, insurance — that the state deliberately took direct ownership/control of, on the belief that leaving them to private capital (domestic or foreign) would be either insufficient in scale or contrary to national developmental priorities.

Major milestones of public sector expansion

Table 6.1 — Key nationalization/public sector milestones
YearEvent
1955Imperial Bank of India nationalized/reconstituted into the State Bank of India
1956Life Insurance Corporation (LIC) formed by nationalizing private insurers
1969Nationalization of 14 major commercial banks (a further 6 in 1980) — aimed at redirecting credit toward priority sectors (agriculture, small industry) rather than only large urban industrial borrowers

Exam angle
Bank nationalization (1969, 1980) is one of the most tested public-sector milestones — remember it was explicitly justified on "social control"/priority-sector lending grounds (extending credit to agriculture and small-scale industry, previously underserved by private banks focused on large urban clients), not simply ideological state expansion for its own sake.
07 — STRAINS

Crises of the Planning Era

  • Slow ("Hindu rate of") growth — average growth of only ~3.5% for decades, widely attributed to over-regulation, licensing inefficiency, and limited competition (see National Income notes)
  • Recurrent Balance of Payments pressures — import-substitution strategy still required importing capital goods/technology and, periodically, food (before the Green Revolution), straining scarce foreign exchange; the 1966 devaluation was one notable episode
  • Fiscal deficits widening through the 1980s, financed increasingly by borrowing (including external commercial borrowing), building unsustainable debt-service obligations
  • Inefficiency of the public sector — many public sector enterprises ran chronic losses, absorbing further fiscal resources rather than generating surpluses for reinvestment
  • "Licence-Permit-Quota Raj" criticised for stifling competition, discouraging efficiency, and creating rent-seeking opportunities
The road to 1991
By the late 1980s, India was running twin deficits — rising fiscal deficits domestically and a worsening current account — financed increasingly through external borrowing, setting the stage for the acute 1991 Balance of Payments crisis (Section 8).
Part C

The New Economic Policy, 1991

08 — THE TRIGGER

Background: The 1991 Balance of Payments Crisis

Proximate causes

  • Gulf War (1990-91) — sharp oil price spike raised India's import bill; remittances from Indian workers in the Gulf region also disrupted
  • Years of fiscal profligacy and mounting external commercial borrowing through the 1980s had left India with a heavy external debt-service burden
  • Political instability (frequent changes of government in the late 1980s-early 1990s) undermined investor confidence
  • Foreign exchange reserves collapsed to a critical level — reportedly enough to cover only about two to three weeks of imports by mid-1991
  • India's sovereign credit rating was downgraded, making fresh external borrowing even harder/costlier
The gold pledge
In an extraordinary, widely-cited emergency measure, India physically pledged a portion of its gold reserves (transported to the Bank of England and the Union Bank of Switzerland) in 1991 to raise emergency foreign exchange loans and avert a sovereign default — a powerful symbol of just how severe the crisis had become.
Exam angle
The "reserves down to about two weeks of imports" and "gold pledged abroad" facts are near-guaranteed exam reference points for the severity of the 1991 crisis — remember the trigger was a Balance of Payments/external sector crisis, not primarily a domestic banking or currency crisis in the way some other countries' reform episodes were triggered.
09 — THE FRAMEWORK

New Economic Policy, 1991: The LPG Framework

New Economic Policy (NEP), 1991
A sweeping package of structural reforms launched under Prime Minister P.V. Narasimha Rao and Finance Minister Dr. Manmohan Singh (with technocratic input including from the IMF/World Bank as a condition of the emergency loan), marking a decisive shift away from the planning-era mixed-economy model toward market-oriented reform — commonly summarised by the acronym LPG.
Table 9.1 — The three pillars of LPG
PillarCore idea
LiberalizationDismantling the Licence-Permit-Quota Raj — abolishing industrial licensing for most industries, removing many restrictions on private investment and production decisions
PrivatizationReducing the role/scope of the public sector — disinvestment (partial sale of government equity) in public sector enterprises, opening previously reserved sectors to private participation
GlobalizationOpening the economy to international trade and capital flows — reducing tariffs, easing restrictions on foreign investment (FDI/FII), integrating India more closely into world markets
Exam angle
"New Economic Policy" (capital letters, a specific named 1991 policy package) should not be confused with generic "new economic policies" in other contexts — in Indian usage, especially in CUET PG-level questions, NEP almost always refers specifically to the 1991 reforms.
10 — INDUSTRIAL REFORM

Industrial Policy, 1991

  • Abolition of industrial licensing for all but a very short list of industries (initially retained for reasons of security, strategic concern, or environmental hazard — e.g., defence equipment, certain hazardous chemicals)
  • Drastic reduction in the list of industries reserved exclusively for the public sector — from 17 industries (under earlier policy) down to a handful (initially 8, later reduced further over subsequent years)
  • Automatic approval for foreign investment/technology agreements up to specified equity limits in many industries, replacing case-by-case government clearance
  • MRTP Act (Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act) restrictions eased — removing the requirement for large firms ("MRTP companies") to seek special government permission before expanding capacity
11 — EXTERNAL SECTOR

Trade & Exchange Rate Reforms

  • Rupee devaluation (in two steps, July 1991) to correct overvaluation and improve export competitiveness
  • Gradual transition from a fixed/managed exchange rate toward a more market-determined exchange rate — the Liberalized Exchange Rate Management System (LERMS, 1992) introduced a dual/partial convertibility mechanism, followed by full current account convertibility of the rupee by 1994
  • Substantial reduction in import tariffs — peak customs duty rates cut sharply over subsequent years from very high (over 200% in some cases) levels
  • Removal of most quantitative restrictions (QRs) on imports over time
  • Liberalization of rules for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and Foreign Institutional Investment (FII), allowing much greater foreign capital participation than the pre-1991 regime permitted
12 — FINANCIAL & FISCAL REFORM

Financial Sector & Fiscal Reforms

  • Narasimham Committee (1991, and again 1998) recommendations on banking sector reform — reducing statutory pre-emptions (CRR/SLR), strengthening prudential norms (capital adequacy, income recognition/asset classification), and greater operational autonomy for banks
  • Capital market reforms — establishment of SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) with statutory powers (1992) as an independent capital market regulator, following the Harshad Mehta securities scam, which itself became a symbol of the risks of an unreformed, poorly-supervised financial system
  • Gradual fiscal consolidation efforts — reducing the fiscal deficit as a share of GDP, reducing/rationalising subsidies (though only partially and gradually, given political sensitivities), broadening the tax base and simplifying tax structures (reduction of peak income tax rates, moves toward a more rationalised indirect tax structure — a process that would eventually culminate much later in the Goods and Services Tax, GST, 2017)
Exam angle
SEBI's establishment (1988 administratively, given statutory powers in 1992) is often linked directly to the Harshad Mehta stock market scam (1992) — a useful cause-effect pairing for exam answers connecting financial-sector reform to a specific triggering event.
13 — ASSESSMENT

Assessing the NEP: Gains & Criticisms

Table 13.1 — The balanced assessment
Argued gainsArgued criticisms/limitations
Sharp acceleration in GDP growth over subsequent decades (from ~3.5-5.5% to 6-9% ranges in various periods)Growth has often been criticised as less employment-intensive ("jobless growth" debates) — see the Occupational Structure notes on the structural transformation puzzle
Sharp rise in foreign exchange reserves and resilience to subsequent external shocks (contrast the comfortable reserve position in later crises with the 1991 near-default)Rising inequality debates — growth benefits perceived as unevenly distributed across regions, sectors, and income groups
Growth of globally competitive Indian firms in IT/services and some manufacturing sectorsAgriculture was comparatively neglected in the reform agenda for years, contributing to continued agrarian distress in various periods
Consumer choice/product availability expanded dramatically (end of chronic shortages typical of the licence-raj era)Public sector reform/disinvestment proceeded slowly and unevenly compared to original ambitions; many loss-making PSUs persisted for decades
India's greater integration into and resilience within the global economyIncreased exposure to global financial volatility (capital flow reversals, contagion risk) as a cost of greater openness
Exam angle
A well-rounded answer on "assess the impact of the NEP" should avoid a purely celebratory or purely critical tone — the expected structure is usually: acknowledge the clear growth acceleration and crisis-resilience gains, while noting genuine, still-debated concerns about employment intensity, inequality, and the incomplete nature of public-sector and agricultural reform.

16 — SELF-TEST

Practice MCQs

Q1. The "Mahalanobis Strategy" refers to the priority given to: Recall
(a) Consumer goods industries   (b) Basic and heavy (capital goods) industries   (c) Agricultural exports   (d) Small-scale/cottage industry exclusively

Answer: (b) — adopted in the Second Five Year Plan (1956-61).
Q2. Which Industrial Policy Resolution classified industries into Schedules A, B, and C? Recall
(a) 1948   (b) 1956   (c) 1977   (d) 1991

Answer: (b) — the Industrial Policy Resolution of 1956, the foundational statement of India's socialistic mixed-economy pattern.
Q3. The primary justification for bank nationalization in 1969 was: Application
(a) To eliminate all private banking entirely   (b) To redirect credit toward priority sectors like agriculture and small industry, previously underserved by private banks   (c) To increase foreign investment in banking   (d) To reduce government control over credit

Answer: (b).
Q4. The immediate trigger for the 1991 Balance of Payments crisis included: Conceptual
(a) A stock market crash   (b) The Gulf War oil price shock, combined with years of fiscal and external debt buildup   (c) A sudden trade surplus   (d) Bank nationalization

Answer: (b).
Q5. In 1991, India's foreign exchange reserves fell to a level sufficient to cover approximately: Recall
(a) Six months of imports   (b) One year of imports   (c) Two to three weeks of imports   (d) Five years of imports

Answer: (c) — reflecting the severity of the crisis, which led to the pledging of gold reserves abroad.
Q6. "LPG" in the context of India's 1991 reforms stands for: Recall
(a) Liquid Petroleum Gas policy   (b) Liberalization, Privatization, Globalization   (c) Land, Planning, Growth   (d) Licence, Permit, Grant

Answer: (b).
Q7. SEBI was granted statutory regulatory powers in 1992, largely in response to: Application
(a) The Gulf War   (b) The Harshad Mehta securities/stock market scam   (c) Bank nationalization   (d) The Green Revolution

Answer: (b).
Q8. Which of the following is a genuine "continuity" from the colonial to the independent Indian economy, rather than a change? Conceptual
(a) Centralized Five Year Plans   (b) A workforce still overwhelmingly concentrated in low-productivity agriculture   (c) End of the Home Charges   (d) Abolition of zamindari in law

Answer: (b) — a structural feature that persisted well beyond 1947 despite policy changes.
Q9. The Industrial Policy of 1991 reduced the number of industries reserved exclusively for the public sector from 17 to approximately: Recall
(a) 15   (b) 8   (c) 0   (d) 25

Answer: (b) — later reduced further in subsequent years.
Q10. A commonly cited criticism of India's post-1991 growth is that it has been: Application
(a) Too slow compared to the pre-1991 period   (b) Relatively less employment-intensive ("jobless growth" concerns)   (c) Entirely agriculture-led   (d) Free of any inequality concerns

Answer: (b) — connecting to the broader structural transformation debate covered in the Occupational Structure notes.
Q11. Full current account convertibility of the Indian rupee was achieved in: Recall
(a) 1991   (b) 1992   (c) 1994   (d) 2000

Answer: (c) — following the transitional LERMS mechanism introduced in 1992.
Q12. The Narasimham Committee's recommendations primarily addressed reform in which sector? Recall
(a) Agriculture   (b) Banking and the financial sector   (c) Railways   (d) Education

Answer: (b) — recommendations delivered in 1991 and again in 1998.
