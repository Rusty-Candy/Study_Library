01 — FOUNDATION

Meaning & the Circular Flow of Income

National Income Accounting
The systematic set of methods and rules by which a nation measures its total output, income, and expenditure over a specific period (usually a year) — the accounting framework underlying all macroeconomic aggregates.

The Circular Flow of Income

The economy is modelled as a circular flow of money and goods/services between sectors. In every complete circuit, aggregate output = aggregate income = aggregate expenditure — the three ways of counting the same thing.

From two-sector to four-sector economy

Table 1.1 — Progressive realism of the circular flow model
ModelSectors includedNew leakages / injections introduced
Two-sectorHouseholds + FirmsNone (closed loop; assumes all income is spent)
Three-sector+ GovernmentLeakage: Taxes (T)  |  Injection: Government spending (G)
Four-sector+ Rest of the WorldLeakage: Imports (M)  |  Injection: Exports (X)
(Also, within 2-sector)Financial sector impliedLeakage: Savings (S)  |  Injection: Investment (I)
Equilibrium condition (four-sector, leakages = injections)
S + T + M = I + G + X
02 — KEY AGGREGATES

Core Macroeconomic Aggregates — Overview

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)
The market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country in a given year, regardless of who owns the factors of production (residents or foreigners).
Gross National Product (GNP)
The market value of all final goods and services produced by a country's normal residents (nationals), whether located within the country or abroad, in a given year.
Domestic ↔ National conversion
GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)
NFIA = Factor income earned by residents from abroad − Factor income earned by non-residents within the domestic territory
Exam angle
Domestic = territory-based (where production happens); National = residency/ownership-based (who owns the factors). A country with large numbers of citizens working abroad (remittances) typically has GNP > GDP; a country hosting large foreign-owned production (e.g., MNC factories) typically has GDP > GNP.
03 — THE AGGREGATES LADDER

From GDP to National Income — the Full Ladder

Market Price vs Factor Cost

Conversion
Market Price (mp) = Factor Cost (fc) + Indirect Taxes − Subsidies
⟹ Factor Cost = Market Price − Net Indirect Taxes  (where Net Indirect Tax = Indirect Tax − Subsidy)

Market price is what the buyer pays (includes taxes, net of subsidies); factor cost is what accrues to the factors of production (wages, rent, interest, profit) — the two differ exactly by net indirect taxes.

Gross vs Net

Conversion
Net = Gross − Depreciation (Consumption of Fixed Capital)
Depreciation
The estimated loss in value of fixed capital (machinery, buildings) due to wear and tear, obsolescence, and the passage of time during the production process.

04 — HOUSEHOLD-LEVEL INCOME

Personal Income & Disposable Income

Personal Income (PI)
The actual income received by households/individuals, as distinct from National Income (which is income earned, whether or not it's actually received by households, e.g. undistributed corporate profits are earned but not received).
From National Income to Personal Income
PI = NI − (Corporate/Undistributed Profits + Corporate Tax + Social Security Contributions) + Transfer Payments
Transfer Payments
Payments made without any corresponding contribution to current production — e.g., pensions, unemployment benefits, scholarships, old-age assistance. These are excluded from National Income but included in Personal Income (received by households) and in Government expenditure accounting distinctly from purchases of goods/services.
Disposable Income (DI)
DI = PI − Direct Personal Taxes
DI = Consumption (C) + Savings (S)   (the household's actual spendable income)

Per Capita Income

Formula
Per Capita Income = National Income / Total Population

A widely used (if imperfect) indicator of average living standards — imperfect because it says nothing about income distribution (inequality) or non-market welfare.

05 — MEASUREMENT

The Three Methods of Measuring National Income

Because output = income = expenditure in the circular flow, national income can be measured from three independent, equivalent vantage points.

Table 5.1 — Overview of the three methods
MethodAlso calledMeasuresSums up
Product / Value Added MethodOutput methodValue of output producedGross Value Added at each stage of production, across all sectors
Income MethodFactor income methodIncome earned by factors of productionRent + Wages + Interest + Profit (factor payments)
Expenditure MethodFinal expenditure methodSpending on final goods/servicesC + I + G + (X − M)
Exam angle
All three methods must yield the same total National Income (in principle) — this identity itself ("output = income = expenditure") is frequently tested as a standalone conceptual question.
06 — METHOD 1

Product / Value Added Method

Gross Value Added (GVA)
GVA = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption (cost of raw materials/inputs bought from other firms)

Summing the value added at every stage of production (rather than the full sale value at each stage) avoids double counting.

Table 6.1 — Worked example: bread production chain
StageSale Value (₹)Cost of Intermediate Inputs (₹)Value Added (₹)
Farmer sells wheat to Miller50050
Miller sells flour to Baker805030
Baker sells bread to Retailer1208040
Retailer sells bread to consumer15012030
Total (sum of sale values)400
Total Value Added (= correct GDP contribution)150
Double Counting
Simply summing all sale values (₹400) massively overstates true output — it counts the wheat's value four times over. The value-added approach (₹150, exactly equal to the final retail price) is the correct measure and automatically avoids this error.
Formula
GDPmp = Σ Gross Value Added at market price, across all sectors/industries of the economy

Sectors typically classified as: Primary (agriculture, mining), Secondary (manufacturing, construction), Tertiary (services).

07 — METHOD 2

Income Method

Formula
National Income (NNPfc) = Compensation of Employees (wages, salaries, benefits) + Rent + Interest + Profit + Mixed Income of Self-Employed
Table 7.1 — Components of factor income
ComponentFactorIncludes
Compensation of EmployeesLabourWages, salaries, employer's social security contributions, wages/salaries in kind
RentLandActual rent + imputed rent of owner-occupied houses
InterestCapitalNet interest received by households on loans given for productive purposes
ProfitEntrepreneurshipCorporate tax + dividends + undistributed profits
Mixed IncomeSelf-employed / unorganised sectorCombines wage, rent, interest, profit elements that can't be separated (farmers, small shopkeepers, professionals)
Items excluded from the income method
Transfer payments (pensions, scholarships), windfall gains (lottery winnings), sale of second-hand goods, sale/purchase of financial assets (shares, bonds) — none of these represent payment for current production.
08 — METHOD 3

Expenditure Method

Formula (the famous GDP identity)
GDPmp = C + I + G + (X − M)
Table 8.1 — Components of aggregate expenditure
ComponentSymbolMeaning
Private Final Consumption ExpenditureCHousehold spending on final goods and services
Gross Domestic Capital Formation (Investment)ISpending on capital goods + change in inventories (both by private & public sector)
Government Final Consumption ExpenditureGGovernment spending on final goods/services (excludes transfer payments)
Net Exports(X − M)Exports minus Imports — captures net foreign demand for domestic output
Why subtract imports (M)?
C, I, and G as typically measured include spending on both domestic and imported goods. Subtracting M removes the foreign-produced portion, ensuring GDP captures only domestically produced output.
Exam angle
Only spending on final goods and services counts — spending on intermediate goods is excluded (to avoid double counting, mirroring the value-added method's logic). Purely financial transactions (buying shares/bonds) and transfer payments are excluded from all three methods.
09 — REAL VS NOMINAL

Nominal GDP, Real GDP & the GDP Deflator

Nominal GDP
GDP measured at current year prices — reflects changes in both output and price level.
Real GDP
GDP measured at constant (base year) prices — reflects changes in output alone, stripping out the effect of price changes (inflation).
GDP Deflator
GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100
Real GDP = (Nominal GDP / GDP Deflator) × 100

The GDP deflator is a broader price index than the CPI — it covers all domestically produced goods and services (not just a fixed consumer "basket"), and it is implicitly reweighted every year based on current output composition (unlike CPI's fixed basket).

Table 9.1 — Worked example
YearNominal GDP (₹ cr)Real GDP (₹ cr, base=Year 1 prices)GDP Deflator
Year 1 (base)1,0001,000100
Year 21,2001,050114.3

Interpretation: nominal GDP grew 20%, but real output grew only 5% — the rest (≈14.3%) reflects price-level increase (inflation), as captured by the deflator.

Exam angle
CPI vs GDP Deflator is a classic comparison: CPI tracks a fixed basket of consumer goods (including imports); the GDP Deflator tracks all domestically produced goods (excludes imports, includes capital goods and government spending) with a changing basket each year.
10 — MEASUREMENT ISSUES

Difficulties & Precautions in Measuring National Income

Conceptual/practical difficulties

  • Non-monetary transactions — subsistence farming, domestic/household services (e.g., a homemaker's unpaid work) are real production but escape market measurement
  • Unreported / informal / "black" economy — significant in developing countries; systematically under-counted
  • Illegal activities — excluded by convention, even if economically substantial
  • Owner-occupied housing — requires an imputed rent estimate (what the owner would pay to rent an equivalent house) to avoid inconsistency
  • Government non-market services (defence, administration) — valued at cost (since there's no market price), which can misstate true value/efficiency
  • Data/statistical reliability — especially in the unorganised sector
  • Depreciation estimation — no precise market test; involves accounting judgement

Precautions to avoid errors

  • Avoid double counting — count only final goods (expenditure method) or only value added (product method)
  • Exclude transfer payments
  • Exclude sale of second-hand goods (no new production; only include the dealer's margin/commission, which is a current service)
  • Exclude purely financial transactions (share/bond trading, gifts) — no corresponding production
  • Include imputed values (owner-occupied housing rent, self-consumed farm output) consistently
11 — BEYOND THE NUMBERS

GDP as a Measure of Welfare — Limitations

GDP is NOT a complete welfare measure
A rising GDP does not automatically mean rising social welfare, because GDP ignores several dimensions that matter for wellbeing.
  • Income distribution — GDP says nothing about inequality; a rising average can mask a worsening distribution
  • Composition of output — GDP treats a rupee of arms spending the same as a rupee of healthcare spending
  • Externalities — pollution, environmental degradation from production are not subtracted (indeed, cleanup spending adds to GDP)
  • Non-market welfare activities — leisure, unpaid domestic/care work, volunteering are excluded
  • Quality-of-life factors — health, education outcomes, political freedom are not captured
  • Defensive/regrettable expenditures — e.g., spending on security due to rising crime boosts GDP without boosting true welfare
Green GNP / Green GDP
An adjusted measure that subtracts the estimated cost of environmental degradation and depletion of natural resources from conventional GNP/GDP — an attempt to reflect sustainable income.

Alternative/supplementary measures

  • Human Development Index (HDI) — combines income, education, and health/longevity
  • Genuine Progress Indicator (GPI), Gross National Happiness (GNH) — broader welfare-oriented composite indices
12 — INDIAN CONTEXT

National Income Accounting in India

  • National Statistical Office (NSO) (under MoSPI — Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation) compiles India's National Accounts Statistics; the Central Statistics Office (CSO) and NSSO were merged to form NSO in 2019
  • India shifted its national accounts base year to 2011-12 (from the earlier 2004-05 base) in the 2015 revision
  • India adopted Gross Value Added (GVA) at basic prices as the primary measure alongside GDP, aligning more closely with the UN's System of National Accounts (SNA) 2008 framework
  • GVA at basic prices → GDP at market prices: GDP = GVA at basic prices + Product Taxes − Product Subsidies
Exam angle
CUET PG occasionally tests current-affairs-adjacent facts like the base year in use, or which body compiles national accounts — worth keeping an eye on official MoSPI/NSO updates closer to the exam.

15 — SELF-TEST

Practice MCQs

Click each question to reveal the answer & explanation.

Q1. GNP differs from GDP by: Recall
(a) Depreciation   (b) Net Factor Income from Abroad   (c) Net Indirect Taxes   (d) Transfer payments

Answer: (b) — GNP = GDP + NFIA.
Q2. Which of the following is correctly excluded from National Income estimation? Assertion-set
I. Sale of second-hand cars
II. Wages paid to a domestic cook
III. Old-age pension payments
IV. Dividends paid by a company

(a) I & III only   (b) I, III only (II and IV are included)   (c) All four   (d) II & IV only

Answer: (b) — II (wages, a factor payment for current production) and IV (dividends, a form of profit distribution) are included; I (no new production) and III (a transfer payment) are excluded.
Q3. The value-added method avoids double counting by: Conceptual
(a) Counting only the final seller's output   (b) Subtracting the cost of intermediate consumption at each stage of production   (c) Ignoring the informal sector   (d) Using only import data

Answer: (b) — GVA = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption at every stage.
Q4. In the expenditure method, why are imports (M) subtracted? Conceptual
(a) Imports are illegal to count   (b) C, I, and G include spending on both domestic and imported goods, so M must be removed to isolate domestically produced output   (c) Imports always exceed exports   (d) To adjust for inflation

Answer: (b).
Q5. Real GDP differs from Nominal GDP because Real GDP: Recall
(a) Uses current year prices   (b) Uses constant (base year) prices, isolating quantity/output change from price change   (c) Excludes government spending   (d) Is always higher than Nominal GDP

Answer: (b).
Q6. The GDP Deflator is calculated as: Numerical
(a) Real GDP / Nominal GDP × 100   (b) Nominal GDP / Real GDP × 100   (c) Nominal GDP − Real GDP   (d) (Nominal GDP + Real GDP)/2

Answer: (b).
Q7. Which of these correctly converts Market Price to Factor Cost? Recall
(a) Factor Cost = Market Price + Net Indirect Tax   (b) Factor Cost = Market Price − Net Indirect Tax   (c) Factor Cost = Market Price × Net Indirect Tax   (d) They are always identical

Answer: (b) — Net Indirect Tax = Indirect Taxes − Subsidies.
Q8. Imputed rent of owner-occupied houses is: Application
(a) Excluded from National Income, since no actual payment is made   (b) Included in National Income as an estimated value, to maintain consistency with rented houses   (c) Counted as a transfer payment   (d) Counted twice, once under rent and once under profit

Answer: (b) — otherwise, identical housing services would be valued differently depending on tenancy status alone.
Q9. Which of the following is a key limitation of GDP as a welfare measure? Conceptual
(a) It is too difficult to calculate   (b) It does not account for income distribution, non-market activities, or environmental externalities   (c) It only includes agricultural output   (d) It cannot be compared across years

Answer: (b).
Q10. Personal Income differs from National Income mainly because: Conceptual
(a) National Income includes transfer payments while Personal Income excludes them   (b) Some income is earned but not received by households (e.g., undistributed corporate profits, corporate tax), while transfer payments are received but not earned from current production   (c) They are always numerically equal   (d) Personal Income includes depreciation while National Income excludes it

Answer: (b).
Q11. India's national accounts currently use which base year? Current Affairs
(a) 2004-05   (b) 2011-12   (c) 2015-16   (d) 1999-2000

Answer: (b) — revised in 2015, shifting the base from 2004-05 to 2011-12.
Q12. "Green GNP" is a concept that: Application
(a) Measures only agricultural income   (b) Adjusts GNP downward for environmental degradation and natural resource depletion   (c) Is identical to Nominal GNP   (d) Measures foreign exchange reserves

Answer: (b).
